\section*{A--B. Counting Rules, Arrangements, and Selections}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{definitionbox}{Sum and product rules}
Use the \term{sum rule} for disjoint alternatives: $m+n$ ways to do one of
two tasks. Use the \term{product rule} for a sequence of choices: $mn$ ways
when every first choice is followed by $n$ second choices. If alternatives
overlap, use inclusion--exclusion:
\[
|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|.
\]
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{workedbox}{A.1: disjoint alternatives}
\textbf{Problem.} A cafe offers $5$ soups and $4$ salads. A lunch includes either one soup or one salad, but not both. How many lunches are possible? Name the counting rule.

\textbf{Worked solution.} The soup lunches and salad lunches are disjoint alternatives. By the sum rule, add their counts: $5+4$.

\textbf{Answer.} $9$ lunches, by the sum rule.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{A.2: successive positions}
\textbf{Problem.} A password contains two uppercase English letters followed by three decimal digits. Repetition is allowed. How many passwords are possible? Give an unsimplified product and name the counting rule.

\textbf{Worked solution.} There are $26$ choices for each letter and $10$ for each digit. Each choice can follow every earlier choice, so the product rule gives $26\cdot26\cdot10\cdot10\cdot10$.

\textbf{Answer.} $26^2 10^3$ passwords, by the product rule.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{A.3: functions}
\textbf{Problem.} Let $A=\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ and $B=\{1,2,\ldots,8\}$. How many functions $f:A\to B$ exist? Identify the sequence of choices being counted.

\textbf{Worked solution.} For each of the five labeled inputs, choose any one of the eight images in $B$. Images may repeat: an arbitrary function need not be injective. Multiply the five independent choices.

\textbf{Answer.} $8^5$ functions; the five choices specify $f(1),\ldots,f(5)$.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{A.4: overlap}
\textbf{Problem.} If finite sets satisfy $|A|=19$, $|B|=20$, and $|A\cap B|=8$, find $|A\cup B|$. Write the formula and name the principle.

\textbf{Worked solution.} Adding $|A|+|B|$ counts every element of $A\cap B$ twice. Inclusion--exclusion removes one copy: $|A\cup B|=|A|+|B|-|A\cap B|=19+20-8$.

\textbf{Answer.} $|A\cup B|=31$, by inclusion--exclusion.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{definitionbox}{Generalized pigeonhole principle}
Placing $N$ objects into $k$ boxes guarantees some box contains at least
\[
\left\lceil\frac Nk\right\rceil
\]
objects.

\textbf{Problem (A.5).} A hash table maps $40$ distinct keys to $20$ possible hash values. What bucket occupancy is guaranteed? State the generalized pigeonhole calculation and explain why no larger occupancy is guaranteed.

\textbf{Worked solution.} Treat keys as objects and hash values as boxes. The guaranteed occupancy is $\lceil40/20\rceil=2$. Two keys per bucket is a possible distribution, so occupancy $3$ is not forced.

\textbf{Answer.} At least one bucket contains at least $2$ keys; no larger bound is guaranteed.
\end{definitionbox}


\begin{definitionbox}{Permutations and combinations}
When order matters, select and arrange:
\[
P(n,r)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}.
\]
When order does not matter, select:
\[
\binom nr=\frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}.
\]
For repeated object types with multiplicities $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ totaling $n$,
the number of distinct arrangements is $n!/(n_1!\cdots n_t!)$.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{workedbox}{B.1: committees}
\textbf{Problem.} A committee of $3$ students is selected from $10$ students. How many committees are possible? Give a binomial-coefficient expression and explain why order does not matter.

\textbf{Worked solution.} A committee is a three-element subset. Listing its members in a different order does not create another committee. Thus choose three of ten without assigning roles.

\textbf{Answer.} $\binom{10}{3}$ committees.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{B.2: labeled offices}
\textbf{Problem.} A president and a vice president are selected from $10$ students, with no student holding both offices. How many outcomes are possible? Give a permutation or product expression and explain why order matters.

\textbf{Worked solution.} Choose the president in $10$ ways, then a different vice president in $9$ ways. Exchanging their offices changes the outcome, so the roles are ordered.

\textbf{Answer.} $P(10,2)=10\cdot9=90$ outcomes.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{B.3: repeated letters}
\textbf{Problem.} How many distinct strings can be made by reordering the letters of \texttt{BANANA}? Give the appropriate multinomial expression.

\textbf{Worked solution.} First pretend the six letters are distinct, giving $6!$ orders. The three A's can be permuted in $3!$ ways and the two N's in $2!$ ways without changing the visible string. Divide by these duplicate counts.

\textbf{Answer.} $6!/(3!2!)=60$ strings.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{B.4: choosing positions}
\textbf{Problem.} How many bit strings of length $8$ contain exactly three $1$s? Give a binomial-coefficient expression and identify what is being selected.

\textbf{Worked solution.} Choose the three positions occupied by $1$s. Every remaining position is forced to be $0$, and each position subset determines exactly one string.

\textbf{Answer.} $\binom83=56$ strings.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{definitionbox}{Binomial theorem}
\[
(x+y)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk x^{n-k}y^k.
\]
\textbf{Problem (B.5).} Find the coefficient of $x^3$ in $(1+x)^8$. State the binomial-theorem term that determines your answer.

\textbf{Worked solution.} The term with $k=3$ is $\binom83 1^5x^3$. Equivalently, choose the three factors that contribute $x$; all other factors contribute $1$.

\textbf{Answer.} The coefficient is $\binom83=56$.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{warningbox}{Frequent A--B errors}
Do not add sequential choices or multiply overlapping alternatives. Decide
whether changing order creates a new outcome before choosing $P(n,r)$ or
$\binom nr$. Divide only for genuinely indistinguishable repetitions.
\end{warningbox}

\begin{checklistbox}{A--B readiness}
Can I name the rule, show an unsimplified expression, explain order, and state
what each factor or selected position represents?
\end{checklistbox}
\end{multicols}

\section*{C. Constrained Counting}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{methodbox}{Four reliable strategies}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \term{Build directly}: multiply successive legal choices.
  \item \term{Split into disjoint cases}: count each case, then add.
  \item \term{Use a complement}: total minus the forbidden cases.
  \item \term{Choose positions or members}: use binomial coefficients, then
  fill remaining roles.
\end{enumerate}
State the sample objects and why the cases neither overlap nor leave gaps.
\end{methodbox}

\begin{workedbox}{C.1: exact composition}
\textbf{Problem.} How many five-card hands from a standard $52$-card deck contain exactly two hearts? A hand is an unordered selection without replacement; the deck has $13$ hearts and $39$ nonhearts. Give an unsimplified product of binomial coefficients.

\textbf{Worked solution.} \textbf{1. Translate ``exactly'':} a five-card hand with exactly two hearts has
two hearts and three nonhearts. \textbf{2. Choose within each category:}
\[
\binom{13}{2}\binom{39}{3}.
\]
\textbf{3. Justify the product:} each qualifying hand has one unique pair of
hearts and triple of nonhearts, so multiply the two unordered selections.

\textbf{Answer.} $\binom{13}{2}\binom{39}{3}$ hands.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{C.2: count by complement}
\textbf{Problem.} How many bit strings of length $8$ contain at least two $1$s? Use a complement and show the subtracted cases.

\textbf{Worked solution.} \textbf{1. Count all:} there are $2^8$ eight-bit strings.
\textbf{2. Identify the complement:} ``at least two $1$s'' fails only for zero
or one $1$. \textbf{3. Subtract the disjoint forbidden cases:}
\[
2^8-\binom80-\binom81.
\]
The complement is shorter because it has only these two cases.

\textbf{Answer.} $2^8-\binom80-\binom81=247$ strings.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{C.3: mixed committee}
\textbf{Problem.} A group of $5$ students is selected from disjoint pools of $12$ computer-science majors and $8$ mathematics majors. How many groups contain exactly $3$ computer-science majors? Give an unsimplified product of binomial coefficients.

\textbf{Worked solution.} A five-person group uses disjoint pools of twelve CS majors and eight math
majors. Exactly three CS majors therefore requires two math majors. Choose
the two unordered subsets in
\[
\binom{12}{3}\binom82
\]
ways. ``Exactly'' fixes both category counts.

\textbf{Answer.} $\binom{12}{3}\binom82$ groups.
\end{workedbox}


\begin{workedbox}{C.4: injective functions}
\textbf{Problem.} Let $A,B$ be finite sets with $|A|=5$ and $|B|=8$. How many injective functions $f:A\to B$ exist? Give a permutation or product expression and explain the restriction.

\textbf{Worked solution.} For $f:A\to B$ with $|A|=5$ and $|B|=8$, \textbf{1. label the five inputs.}
\textbf{2. Enforce injectivity:} after an output is used, it is unavailable.
The successive choices are
\[
8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4=P(8,5).
\]
\textbf{3. Check overcounting:} input labels already distinguish the positions,
so do not divide by $5!$.

\textbf{Answer.} $P(8,5)=8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4$ injective functions.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{C.5: codes with a first-position rule}
\textbf{Problem.} A six-character code uses uppercase English letters without repetition and must begin with a vowel. Treat $A,E,I,O,U$ as the five vowels. How many codes are possible? Give an unsimplified product.

\textbf{Worked solution.} \textbf{1. Enforce the special position first:} a six-letter code beginning
with a vowel has five first-character choices. \textbf{2. Remove that letter:}
without repetition, the remaining ordered choices are $25,24,23,22,21$:
\[
5\cdot25\cdot24\cdot23\cdot22\cdot21.
\]
Each factor now has a visible job: the first enforces the vowel rule, and the
others enforce no repetition.

\textbf{Answer.} $5\cdot25\cdot24\cdot23\cdot22\cdot21$ codes.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{definitionbox}{Avoiding overcounting}
A counting method is valid only if each desired object is generated exactly
once. Ask:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Can the same outcome arise from two different choices or cases?
  \item Did I assign an order to an unordered object?
  \item Did I forget labels that make positions distinct?
  \item Are my cases exhaustive and disjoint?
\end{itemize}
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{warningbox}{Frequent C errors}
``At least'' usually needs several cases or a complement. ``Without
replacement'' changes later factors. A restriction on the first character
must be enforced before counting unrestricted remaining positions. Never use
$n^r$ for an injective function unless $r\le1$.
\end{warningbox}

\begin{checklistbox}{C readiness}
Can I construct the count two ways, identify possible overlap, justify every
factor, and leave a correct unsimplified expression?
\end{checklistbox}
\end{multicols}

\section*{D. Probability Models}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{definitionbox}{Sample spaces and events}
A \term{sample space} $\Omega$ lists all possible outcomes; an \term{event}
is a subset of $\Omega$. For equally likely outcomes,
\[
P(A)=\frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}.
\]
Always justify equal likelihood. Useful identities are
\begin{align*}
P(A^c)&=1-P(A),\\
P(A\cup B)&=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B),\\
P(A\cap B)&=P(A)P(B\mid A).
\end{align*}
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{workedbox}{D.1: coin and die}
\textbf{Problem.} A fair coin is tossed independently of a fair six-sided die roll. Find the probability that the coin is heads or the die is even (or both). Show the favorable-outcome count or an equivalent probability calculation.

\textbf{Worked solution.} \textbf{Model:} toss a fair coin and roll a fair die independently.
\textbf{Sample space:} there are $2\cdot6=12$ equally likely coin--die pairs.
\textbf{Favorable count:} heads gives $6$, an even die gives $6$, and their
intersection gives $3$. Hence $6+6-3=9$ favorable outcomes and
$P=9/12=3/4$. The subtraction removes the duplicate intersection.

\textbf{Answer.} $3/4$.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{D.2: probability identities}
\textbf{Problem.} Events $A,B$ satisfy $P(A)=0.55$, $P(B)=0.40$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.25$. Find $P(A\cup B)$ and $P(A^c\cap B)$, showing the identities used.

\textbf{Worked solution.} If $P(A)=0.55$, $P(B)=0.40$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.25$, then
\[
P(A\cup B)=0.55+0.40-0.25=0.70.
\]
Also $B=(A\cap B)\mathbin{\dot\cup}(A^c\cap B)$, so
$P(A^c\cap B)=0.40-0.25=0.15$.

\textbf{Answer.} $P(A\cup B)=0.70$ and $P(A^c\cap B)=0.15$.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{D.3: uniform bit strings}
\textbf{Problem.} An $8$-bit string is chosen uniformly from all $2^8$ such strings. Find the probability that it contains exactly three $1$s. Give an exact fraction.

\textbf{Worked solution.} \textbf{Denominator:} $2^8$ equally likely eight-bit strings.
\textbf{Numerator:} choose the three positions containing $1$s, so $\binom83$
strings qualify. Thus
\[
P(\text{exactly three }1\text{s})=\frac{\binom83}{2^8}.
\]
The numerator and denominator must describe the same sample space.

\textbf{Answer.} $\binom83/2^8=7/32$.
\end{workedbox}


\begin{definitionbox}{Conditional probability and independence}
For $P(B)>0$,
\[
P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}.
\]
$A$ and $B$ are independent exactly when
$P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)$, equivalently $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$ when defined.
Independence is a claim to test, not a default assumption.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{workedbox}{D.4: without replacement}
\textbf{Problem.} A bag contains $4$ red and $6$ blue tokens. Two tokens are drawn uniformly, one at a time, without replacement. Find the probability that the first is red and the second is blue. Show the conditional product.

\textbf{Worked solution.} A bag has four red and six blue tokens. \textbf{First draw:} red has
probability $4/10$. \textbf{Update:} after removing a red, nine tokens remain
and all six blue tokens remain. Therefore
\[
\frac4{10}\cdot\frac69.
\]
The second factor is conditional on the first draw; keeping denominator $10$
would describe replacement, not this experiment.

\textbf{Answer.} $(4/10)(6/9)=4/15$.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{D.5: two different conditionals}
\textbf{Problem.} In a class, $24$ students use Python, $18$ use Java, and $10$ use both. One student is chosen uniformly from the class. Let $P$ mean uses Python and $J$ mean uses Java. Find $P(P\mid J)$ and $P(J\mid P)$.

\textbf{Worked solution.} If $24$ students use Python, $18$ use Java, and $10$ use both, then
\[
P(P\mid J)=\frac{10}{18},\qquad
P(J\mid P)=\frac{10}{24}.
\]
The shared numerator is the intersection. The event after the conditioning bar
determines the denominator, so reversing the conditional usually changes the
value.

\textbf{Answer.} $P(P\mid J)=10/18=5/9$; $P(J\mid P)=10/24=5/12$.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{warningbox}{Frequent D errors}
Do not add probabilities for a non-disjoint ``or'' without subtracting the
overlap. Do not keep a denominator unchanged after sampling without
replacement. Do not confuse $P(A\mid B)$ with $P(B\mid A)$ or infer
independence from informal wording.
\end{warningbox}

\begin{checklistbox}{D readiness}
Can I name the sample space, choose the correct denominator, use a complement
or overlap identity, and distinguish conditional probability from
independence?
\end{checklistbox}
\end{multicols}

\section*{E. Modeling, Bayes, Expectation, and Interpretation}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\begin{definitionbox}{Total probability and Bayes' rule}
If $B_1,\ldots,B_m$ partition the sample space, then
\[
P(A)=\sum_i P(A\mid B_i)P(B_i).
\]
Bayes' rule reverses the condition:
\[
P(B_j\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid B_j)P(B_j)}{P(A)}.
\]
The prior or base rate $P(B_j)$ remains part of the numerator.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{workedbox}{E.1: numerical independence test}
\textbf{Problem.} Events $A,B$ satisfy $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.5$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.3$. Determine whether they are independent, showing the numerical test, and then find $P(A\mid B)$.

\textbf{Worked solution.} If $P(A)=0.6$, $P(B)=0.5$, and $P(A\cap B)=0.3$, then
$P(A)P(B)=0.3=P(A\cap B)$, so the events are independent. Consequently
$P(A\mid B)=0.3/0.5=0.6=P(A)$.

\textbf{Answer.} They are independent, and $P(A\mid B)=0.6$.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{E.2: screening and base rates}
\textbf{Problem.} A condition occurs in $1\%$ of a population. A test is positive for $95\%$ of people who have the condition and for $8\%$ of people who do not. For a person chosen at random from this population, find $P(\text{positive})$ and $P(\text{condition}\mid\text{positive})$. Give a one-sentence base-rate interpretation.

\textbf{Worked solution.} Let $C$ be the condition and $+$ a positive test. Then
\[
\underbrace{0.95(0.01)}_{\text{true positives}}=0.0095,
\qquad
\underbrace{0.08(0.99)}_{\text{false positives}}=0.0792.
\]
Add the two disjoint routes: $P(+)=0.0095+0.0792=0.0887$. Then Bayes' rule
keeps the true-positive route in the numerator:
\[
P(C\mid +)=\frac{0.0095}{0.0887}\approx0.107.
\]
Even a sensitive test can yield many false positives when the condition is
rare; the denominator includes both true and false positives.

\textbf{Answer.} $P(+)=0.0887$ and $P(C\mid +)=95/887\approx10.7\%$. Most positive results are false positives because the condition is rare.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{workedbox}{E.3: factory Bayes model}
\textbf{Problem.} Factory A supplies $40\%$ of a store's batteries and $3\%$ of its batteries are defective. Factory B supplies the rest and $1\%$ of its batteries are defective. A battery is selected uniformly from the store's stock and is found defective. Find the probability it came from A, showing total probability and Bayes' rule.

\textbf{Worked solution.} Factory A supplies $40\%$ with defect rate $3\%$; B supplies $60\%$ with rate
$1\%$. First compute both joint routes:
\[
\begin{aligned}
P(A\cap D)&=0.40(0.03)=0.012,\\
P(B\cap D)&=0.60(0.01)=0.006.
\end{aligned}
\]
Thus $P(D)=0.018$ and $P(A\mid D)=0.012/0.018=2/3$.

\textbf{Answer.} $P(A\mid D)=2/3$.
\end{workedbox}


\begin{definitionbox}{Expected value}
For numerical outcomes $x_i$ with probabilities $p_i$,
\[
E[X]=\sum_i x_i p_i.
\]
Use \emph{net} outcomes when a cost is paid. Expected value is a long-run
average, not a guaranteed result from one trial.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{workedbox}{E.4: expected net value}
\textbf{Problem.} A game uses a fair six-sided die. It pays \$10 for a $6$, \$2 for a $4$ or $5$, and \$0 otherwise. A play costs \$2 regardless of the result. Find the expected net value of one play and interpret its sign.

\textbf{Worked solution.} A fair die pays $10$ on a $6$, $2$ on a $4$ or $5$, and $0$ otherwise. With a
$2$ cost, first convert payouts to net outcomes: $8,0,-2$, with probabilities
$1/6,2/6,3/6$. Therefore
\[
E[X]=8\left(\frac16\right)+0\left(\frac26\right)
-2\left(\frac36\right)=\frac13.
\]
The positive sign means an average gain of $\$1/3$ per play over many plays.

\textbf{Answer.} $E[X]=\$1/3$ per play, a positive long-run average gain.
\end{workedbox}

\begin{definitionbox}{Aggregation reversal}
A treatment can have the higher success rate in every subgroup but a lower
overall rate when the groups receive treatments in very different proportions.
Compute each within-group rate and each pooled rate separately. Then explain
which high- or low-success group dominates each pooled denominator; do not say
that arithmetic is contradictory.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{methodbox}{Twenty-minute model-selection routine}
For one problem from each A--E section: name the rule or model first, write an
exact unsimplified expression, then calculate or interpret. Check that each
denominator matches its condition and that each counted outcome appears once.
\end{methodbox}

\begin{warningbox}{Frequent E errors}
Do not ignore base rates, reverse a conditional, or assume independence. In
expected value, subtract costs from every applicable outcome. In aggregated
tables, compare rates rather than raw success counts.
\end{warningbox}

\begin{checklistbox}{Whole-Core readiness}
I can choose among sum, product, cases, complement, permutation, and
combination; justify a probability denominator; compute conditional and Bayes
probabilities; test independence; calculate net expected value; and explain an
aggregation reversal. A live form contains one problem from each A--E section.
\end{checklistbox}
