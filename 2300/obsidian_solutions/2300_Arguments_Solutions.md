# Arguments Core Solutions

These are complete worked solutions to the public **Arguments Core Extended Practice** bank.
They are not answer keys to a live form; supervised paper forms use new scenarios
from the same problem families.

A live attempt lasts 20 minutes and contains five scoring units, one from each
section A–E. Meets is four of five fully correct; Exceeds is five of five.
For Sections B, C, and D, the correct selected answer is the complete response;
no written justification is required. For Sections A and E, students give only
the one or two compact results named in the prompt.

All passages, scenarios, choices, and explanations are original course material.
No official LSAC or LawHub question is reproduced.

## Full-credit and grader guidance

The worked responses below are study models and grader rationales, not the only
acceptable wording and not a statement of how much students must write. In
Sections B, C, and D, the correct selected answer earns full credit; the detailed
explanations below help students study and help graders check the key, but are
not required on an assessment. In Sections A and E, the bold **Expected short
response** is sufficient for full credit; any material that follows it is optional
study explanation, not an additional required assessment part.

The **decisive check** listed for each section is a fast way for a grader to
locate the central reasoning move. Under the course mastery rule, a response is
fully correct when it supplies the compact result explicitly requested by the
printed problem.

> [!TIP] How to study from this page
> 1. Hide the solution and identify the question family (for example, main conclusion, necessary assumption, or causal evaluation).
> 2. Apply the short procedure at the beginning of that section before looking at the choices or model response.
> 3. Reveal the solution and compare the *reasoning step* that decided the problem, not merely the final answer.
> 4. For A and E, practice giving only the one or two requested results. For B, C, and D, practice reaching the selected answer without writing a justification.

## A. Reconstruct and Challenge Natural-Language Arguments

> [!TIP]- Step-by-step method for Section A
> 1. **Find the endpoint:** underline the claim the author ultimately wants accepted.
> 2. **Identify only what was requested:** this may be an intermediate conclusion, a translation, or a validity judgment.
> 3. **Check the logic:** translate conditionals in the licensed direction; for an invalid argument, make every premise true and the conclusion false.
> 4. **Answer compactly:** stop after the one or two requested results.

> [!CHECK]- Minimum full-credit response and decisive checks
> - **A.1:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion. **Decisive check:** the response distinguishes the final recommendation from the claim that supports it.
> - **A.2:** Give the requested translation and one requested consequence or classification. **Decisive check:** the conditional arrow points in the direction licensed by the text.
> - **A.3:** If valid, write *valid* and name the rule; if invalid, write *invalid* and give one countermodel. **Decisive check:** a countermodel makes every premise true and the conclusion false.

### A.1 Argument Structure

State only the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

#### A.1.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The team should archive a build log for every release. After all, archived build logs receive automatic timestamps, and any record with a trustworthy timestamp can be audited after a failure. Archived build logs can therefore be audited after a failure, while an auditable release process makes debugging more reliable.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: archive a build log for every release. Intermediate: archived build logs can be audited after a failure.
>
> **Main conclusion:** The team should archive a build log for every release.
>
> **Intermediate conclusion:** Archived build logs can be audited after a failure.
>
> **Stated premises:**
> 1. Archived build logs receive automatic timestamps.
> 2. Any record with a trustworthy timestamp can be audited after a failure.
> 3. An auditable release process makes debugging more reliable.
>
> The intended chain is archived log $\rightarrow$ trustworthy timestamp $\rightarrow$ auditable after a failure. There is a small unstated factual bridge: **the automatic timestamps on these logs are trustworthy**. “Automatic” alone does not mean “trustworthy.” With that assumption, premises 1 and 2 establish the intermediate conclusion. The intermediate conclusion and premise 3 then support the recommendation, but they do not deductively entail it without a value or policy premise. Identifying the author's intermediate conclusion does not require accepting either unstated assumption.
>
> One plausible bridge premise is: **If an affordable recordkeeping practice makes every release auditable and thereby makes debugging more reliable, the team should adopt that practice for every release.** With that bridge in place, the recommendation follows. Notice that premise 3 does not itself prove that the team should archive logs; it explains why auditability is valuable.

#### A.1.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The city should place its next three bike-share docks beside rail stations. The city wants more residents to combine bicycles with transit, and most bike-share trips already begin or end within two blocks of a station. Moreover, station-area docks are used more often than otherwise similar docks. Those patterns indicate that placing docks near stations increases bike-share use.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: place the next three docks beside rail stations. Intermediate: placing docks near stations increases bike-share use.
>
> The main conclusion is that the city should place its next three docks beside rail stations. The intermediate conclusion is that placing docks near rail stations increases bike-share use. The stated premises concern where trips begin or end, the higher use of comparable station-area docks, and the city's goal of increasing bicycle-transit combinations. The first two premises support the intermediate causal claim; that claim and the stated goal support the recommendation. A bridge is that, other relevant costs being comparable, the city should choose dock locations that advance its stated transportation goal.

#### A.1.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Tutorial videos without captions cannot be used fully by every student. The course should add accurate captions before assigning its tutorial videos. The reason is that materials some students cannot use fully do not satisfy the department's accessibility rule; consequently, uncaptioned tutorial videos do not satisfy that rule.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: add accurate captions before assigning the videos. Intermediate: uncaptioned videos do not satisfy the accessibility rule.
>
> The main conclusion is that the course should add accurate captions before assigning the videos. The intermediate conclusion is that uncaptioned tutorial videos do not satisfy the accessibility rule. The premises are that such videos cannot be used fully by every student and that material some students cannot use fully violates the rule. Those premises jointly support the intermediate conclusion. A bridge to the recommendation is that a course should not assign material that violates the accessibility rule when adding accurate captions would make it compliant.

#### A.1.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The support team should require repeatable steps in every nonemergency bug report. A report with repeatable steps lets an engineer reproduce the failure, and reproducing a failure makes its cause easier to isolate. Reports with repeatable steps therefore make causes easier to isolate. This matters because faster diagnosis shortens outages.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: require repeatable steps in every nonemergency bug report. Intermediate: reports with repeatable steps make causes easier to isolate.
>
> The main conclusion is that every nonemergency bug report should require repeatable steps. The intermediate conclusion is that reports containing such steps make causes easier to isolate. The first two statements form a chain from repeatable steps to reproduction to easier isolation. The statement that faster diagnosis shortens outages is an additional premise. A bridge is that easier isolation produces faster diagnosis and that requirements which reduce outages at reasonable cost should be adopted.

#### A.1.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Because the neighborhood has little existing shade, the city should plant canopy trees along its busiest walking routes. Measurements show that shaded blocks stay cooler during afternoon heat, and lower afternoon temperatures reduce heat-related risk for pedestrians. Increasing street shade can therefore reduce pedestrian heat risk.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: plant canopy trees along the busiest walking routes. Intermediate: increasing street shade can reduce pedestrian heat risk.
>
> The main conclusion recommends planting canopy trees along the busiest walking routes. The intermediate conclusion is that increasing street shade can reduce pedestrian heat risk. The measurement about shaded blocks and the premise connecting lower temperatures to lower risk support that intermediate conclusion. The lack of existing shade is a further premise. A bridge is that canopy trees will materially increase shade on those routes and are an acceptable way to reduce a significant pedestrian risk.

#### A.1.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Small commits reduce recovery time after a defect: focused version-control commits make it possible to reverse one change without discarding unrelated work, and selective reversal shortens recovery. The development team should therefore require each feature to be committed in focused steps.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: require each feature to be committed in focused steps. Intermediate: small commits reduce recovery time after a defect.
>
> **Main conclusion:** The development team should require each feature to be committed in focused steps.
>
> **Intermediate conclusion:** Small commits reduce recovery time after a defect.
>
> **Stated premises:**
> 1. Focused commits make it possible to reverse one change without discarding unrelated work.
> 2. Selective reversal shortens recovery after a defect.
>
> The support is serial rather than independent: focused commits enable selective reversal, and selective reversal shortens recovery. Together those claims support the intermediate conclusion. The intermediate conclusion then supports the policy recommendation.
>
> Two small bridges are implicit. First, committing a feature in focused steps produces the relevant kind of small commits. Second, **when a feasible development practice materially shortens recovery without a comparable cost, the team should require it.** Without that normative bridge, the passage establishes a benefit but not yet a requirement.

#### A.1.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The transit agency should shift the northbound departure five minutes later. Currently, when the eastbound bus arrives after the northbound bus leaves, riders wait nearly thirty minutes. A five-minute timetable shift would make the connection reliable on most days, and reliable connections reduce total travel time.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: shift the northbound departure five minutes later. Intermediate: the shift would make the connection reliable on most days.
>
> The main conclusion recommends delaying the northbound departure by five minutes. The intermediate conclusion is that the shift would make the connection reliable on most days. The premises describe the current missed connection, the effect of the proposed shift, and the travel-time benefit of reliable connections. A bridge is that the small schedule cost does not outweigh the substantial reduction in connection waits, so the agency should implement the change.

#### A.1.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> An offline backup cannot be encrypted by ransomware that reaches only the active network, and a backup that remains unencrypted can restore records after such an attack. The clinic should keep one daily backup disconnected from its network. The relevant intermediate result is that an offline backup protects the ability to restore records.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: keep one daily backup offline. Intermediate: an offline backup protects the clinic's ability to restore records.
>
> The main conclusion is that the clinic should keep one daily backup offline. The intermediate conclusion is that an offline backup protects restoration ability. The premises say the attack cannot encrypt the offline copy and an unencrypted copy can restore records. A bridge is that a daily offline copy will be recent enough to provide meaningful restoration and that the clinic should take a feasible step that protects essential records.

#### A.1.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The library should extend its finals-week hours. Students used the library's late hours heavily during the two pilot weeks, and resources used heavily during peak demand address a demonstrated need. The pilot thus addressed a demonstrated need. Keeping the library open one additional hour during finals would continue that service.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: extend finals-week hours. Intermediate: the pilot addressed a demonstrated need.
>
> The main conclusion recommends extending finals-week hours. The intermediate conclusion is that the pilot addressed a demonstrated need. Heavy pilot use and the general statement about heavy use during peak demand support that intermediate conclusion. The claim that an extra hour would continue the service is another premise. A bridge is that, absent prohibitive cost or harm, a library should continue a service shown to meet a demonstrated finals-period need.

#### A.1.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The utility should offer real-time water meters first in buildings with aging plumbing. Households with such meters detect leaks sooner than households receiving only monthly bills, and earlier detection prevents more water loss. Real-time meters can therefore reduce water lost to leaks.
> **Short response:** State the main conclusion and the intermediate conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: offer real-time meters first in buildings with aging plumbing. Intermediate: the meters can reduce water lost to leaks.
>
> The main conclusion is that the utility should initially offer real-time meters in buildings with aging plumbing. The intermediate conclusion is that the meters can reduce water lost to leaks. The first two premises form a chain from real-time feedback through earlier detection to prevented loss. A bridge is that aging plumbing creates a comparatively high leak risk and that prioritizing a scarce intervention where expected benefit is greatest is justified.

### A.2 Hidden Conditionals

Give the requested translation and the one requested consequence or classification.

#### A.2.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let T mean training is complete and E mean entry is permitted. Entry is permitted only if training is complete.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and write its contrapositive.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $E \rightarrow T$; therefore $\neg T \rightarrow \neg E$.
>
> “Only if” introduces a necessary condition: whenever entry is permitted,
> training must be complete. Thus $E\to T$. Reverse the direction and negate
> both propositions to obtain $\neg T\to\neg E$. Neither $T\to E$ nor a
> supervision requirement is given: a trained person might still be denied entry.

#### A.2.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let P mean a patch is installed, R mean the server restarts, and V mean the vulnerability is removed. Installing the patch and restarting the server guarantees removal.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and name a sufficient condition for V.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $(P \land R) \rightarrow V$; $P \land R$ is sufficient for $V$.
>
> “Guarantees” makes the combined action a sufficient condition. The antecedent
> is $P\land R$, not $P$ or $R$ alone. If both are true, modus ponens gives $V$.
> The rule does **not** say that patching or restarting is necessary: removal
> might also be achieved another way. For example, $P=F,R=F,V=T$ satisfies the rule.

#### A.2.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let M mean a member may vote, D mean dues are current, and A mean the member attended orientation. A member may vote only if dues are current and orientation was attended.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and name one condition necessary for M.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $M \rightarrow (D \land A)$; for example, $D$ is necessary for $M$.
>
> “Only if” points from permission to the required conditions:
> $M\to(D\land A)$. Simplifying its consequent gives $M\to D$ and $M\to A$,
> so either $D$ or $A$ is an acceptable necessary condition. The conjunction
> is also necessary. No sufficient condition for $M$ has been supplied; even
> current dues and attendance together do not establish permission from this
> one-way rule. For example, $M=F,D=T,A=T$ makes the rule true.

#### A.2.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let B mean a file is backed up, C mean it is copied to cloud storage, and L mean it is copied to a local drive. A file is backed up exactly when it has a cloud copy or a local copy.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and state what $\neg B$ implies.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $B \leftrightarrow (C \lor L)$; therefore $\neg B \rightarrow (\neg C \land \neg L)$.
>
> “Exactly when” supplies both directions: $B\to(C\lor L)$ and
> $(C\lor L)\to B$. To answer the second part, contrapose the latter to get
> $\neg B\to\neg(C\lor L)$, then use De Morgan's law:
> $\neg B\to(\neg C\land\neg L)$. Thus a file without backup has neither
> kind of copy. From $B$ alone, however, we cannot tell which kind exists.

#### A.2.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let G mean a student may graduate, C mean all core requirements are complete, and F mean the final form is filed. Completing every core and filing the final form guarantees graduation.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and state whether C alone is sufficient for G.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $(C \land F) \rightarrow G$; no, $C$ alone is not sufficient for $G$.
>
> Both listed actions together guarantee $G$, so the rule is $(C\land F)\to G$.
> It does not license dropping $F$ from the antecedent. To check that $C$ alone
> does not guarantee $G$, use $C=T,F=F,G=F$: the given conditional is true
> while $C\to G$ is false. More precisely, $C$ alone is **not established as
> sufficient by the stated rule**. The rule also does not establish that either
> action is necessary; other paths to graduation have not been ruled out.

#### A.2.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let N mean a notification is sent, H mean the threshold is high, and U mean urgent mode is enabled. A notification is sent only when the threshold is high or urgent mode is enabled.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and write its contrapositive.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $N \rightarrow (H \lor U)$; therefore $(\neg H \land \neg U) \rightarrow \neg N$.
>
> A sent notification requires at least one listed condition, giving
> $N\to(H\lor U)$. Its contrapositive is
> $\neg(H\lor U)\to\neg N$, or $(\neg H\land\neg U)\to\neg N$ by
> De Morgan's law. Neither $H\to N$ nor $U\to N$ is given: satisfying a
> necessary condition need not be enough to trigger the notification.

#### A.2.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let A mean an application is accepted, I mean the interview is passed, and R mean references are approved. Passing the interview and receiving approved references is sufficient for acceptance.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and name a sufficient condition for A.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $(I \land R) \rightarrow A$; $I \land R$ is sufficient for $A$.
>
> “Is sufficient for” puts the combined conditions in the antecedent:
> $(I\land R)\to A$. Whenever both are true, acceptance follows by modus
> ponens. No reverse rule is stated, so we cannot conclude that an accepted
> applicant must have passed this interview or received these references.
> A conjunction can be sufficient without either conjunct being necessary.

#### A.2.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let O mean an event is held outdoors, W mean the forecast is dry, and P mean a permit is approved. The event is held outdoors exactly when the forecast is dry and the permit is approved.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and name one condition necessary for O.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $O \leftrightarrow (W \land P)$; for example, $W$ is necessary for $O$.
>
> “Exactly when” gives $O\leftrightarrow(W\land P)$. Its forward direction
> gives $O\to W$ and $O\to P$, so either $W$ or $P$ answers the necessary-
> condition question. Its reverse direction makes $W\land P$ sufficient.
> Neither conjunct alone is guaranteed sufficient: for example, a dry forecast
> with no permit makes $W$ true and $O$ false while respecting the biconditional.

#### A.2.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let X mean a message is encrypted, K mean the recipient has the key, and D mean the message can be decoded. Possessing the key is sufficient to decode an encrypted message.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule using X and state the sufficient condition for D.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $(X \land K) \rightarrow D$; for an encrypted message, knowing $K$ is sufficient for $D$.
>
> The phrase “an encrypted message” supplies the condition $X$. Therefore the
> unrestricted translation is $(X\land K)\to D$, and $X\land K$ is a sufficient
> condition for $D$. Within the encrypted-message context ($X$ assumed), $K$
> is sufficient. The statement does not make $K$ necessary: it does not rule
> out decoding without the key by some other method. Thus $(X\land D)\to K$
> and $X\to(D\leftrightarrow K)$ must not be added as premises.

#### A.2.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Let R mean a room may be reserved, E mean the requester is eligible, and F mean the room is free. A reservation is permitted if and only if the requester is eligible and the room is free.
>
> **Short response:** Translate the rule and state whether E alone is sufficient for R.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** $R \leftrightarrow (E \land F)$; no, $E$ alone is not sufficient for $R$.
>
> “If and only if” gives $R\leftrightarrow(E\land F)$, so eligibility and a free
> room together are sufficient, and each is necessary. Eligibility alone does
> not guarantee permission: $E=T,F=F,R=F$ satisfies the biconditional. A short
> explanation is “No—the room must also be free.”

### A.3 Validity and Countermodels

If valid, state *valid* and name the rule. If invalid, state *invalid* and give one true-premise/false-conclusion countermodel.

#### A.3.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If both the patch is installed and the server restarts, the vulnerability is removed. If the vulnerability is removed, the compliance audit passes. The patch is installed, and the server restarts. Therefore, the compliance audit passes.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — conjunction followed by modus ponens twice.
>
> Let P mean the patch is installed, R the server restarts, V the vulnerability is removed, and A the audit passes. Numbered form: (1) (P ∧ R) → V; (2) V → A; (3) P; (4) R; conclusion: A. Conjoin (3) and (4), use modus ponens with (1) to derive V, and use modus ponens with (2) to derive A. The argument is valid.

#### A.3.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If the badge is active, then either the door unlocks or the security desk receives an alert. The door did not unlock, and the security desk received no alert. Therefore, the badge is not active.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — De Morgan's law followed by modus tollens.
>
> Let B mean the badge is active, U the door unlocks, and D the desk receives an alert. Numbered form: (1) B → (U ∨ D); (2) ¬U; (3) ¬D; conclusion: ¬B. Premises (2) and (3) imply ¬(U ∨ D) by De Morgan's law. Modus tollens with (1) then yields ¬B. The argument is valid.

#### A.3.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Either the parser rejects the file or the compiler accepts it, but not both. The compiler accepts the file only if its schema is valid. The schema is not valid. Therefore, the parser rejects the file.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — modus tollens followed by disjunctive syllogism.
>
> Let P mean the parser rejects the file, A the compiler accepts it, and S the schema is valid. Numbered form: (1) (P ∨ A) ∧ ¬(P ∧ A); (2) A → S; (3) ¬S; conclusion: P. From (2) and (3), modus tollens gives ¬A. The first conjunct of (1) gives P ∨ A, and disjunctive syllogism yields P. The exclusivity conjunct is accurate but not needed. The argument is valid.

#### A.3.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If the sensor is calibrated, its readings are stable. If the scheduler is active, samples arrive regularly. If readings are stable and samples arrive regularly, the controller can use them. The sensor is calibrated, and the scheduler is active. Therefore, the controller can use the readings.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — modus ponens, conjunction, then modus ponens.
>
> Let C mean calibrated, S stable readings, A an active scheduler, R regular samples, and U usable readings. Numbered form: (1) C → S; (2) A → R; (3) (S ∧ R) → U; (4) C; (5) A; conclusion: U. Modus ponens gives S and R from the first two rules; conjunction gives S ∧ R; a final modus ponens gives U. The argument is valid.

#### A.3.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If the cache is stale, then the integration test fails and the warning log records a mismatch. The integration test failed, and the warning log recorded a mismatch. Therefore, the cache is stale.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Invalid — countermodel $C=\text{false}, F=\text{true}, M=\text{true}$.
>
> Let $C$ mean “the cache is stale,” $F$ mean “the integration test fails,” and $M$ mean “the warning log records a mismatch.” The argument is:
>
> 1. $C \rightarrow (F \land M)$
> 2. $F$
> 3. $M$
> 4. Therefore, $C$.
>
> This is **invalid**. Premises 2 and 3 allow us to infer $F \land M$, but premise 1 says only that a stale cache is one sufficient cause of those observations. It does not say that a stale cache is their only possible cause. The inference affirms the consequent.
>
> A countermodel sets $C$ false, $F$ true, and $M$ true. Then $C \rightarrow (F \land M)$ is true because its antecedent is false, both observational premises are true, and the conclusion is false. A different defect could have caused both observations.
>
> A minimal repair is to add $(F \land M) \rightarrow C$, or equivalently replace premise 1 with $C \leftrightarrow (F \land M)$. With that additional direction, conjunction gives $F \land M$ and modus ponens gives $C$.

#### A.3.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If the room is reserved, the calendar shows it. If the calendar shows the room, the coordinator receives a notice. The room is not reserved. Therefore, the coordinator did not receive a notice.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Invalid — countermodel $R=\text{false}, C=\text{true}, N=\text{true}$.
>
> Let R mean reserved, C shown on the calendar, and N that the coordinator receives a notice. Numbered form: (1) R → C; (2) C → N; (3) ¬R; conclusion: ¬N. It is invalid by denying the antecedent of the chain. A countermodel is R=false, C=true, N=true. Replacing (2) with N → R is a minimal repair, since ¬R and N → R yield ¬N by modus tollens; alternatively, adding N → R repairs the original set.

#### A.3.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Every certified device passed the safety test. No prototype passed the safety test. Unit K is a prototype, although some nonprototype units also failed the test. Therefore, Unit K is not certified.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — universal instantiation followed by modus tollens.
>
> Let C(x) mean certified, S(x) passed the safety test, P(x) prototype, and k denote Unit K. Numbered form: (1) ∀x(C(x) → S(x)); (2) ∀x(P(x) → ¬S(x)); (3) P(k); (4) some nonprototype is not S; conclusion: ¬C(k). From (2) and (3), derive ¬S(k); with the instance C(k) → S(k) of (1), modus tollens yields ¬C(k). The existential aside is irrelevant. The argument is valid.

#### A.3.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If the lab is open, either a technician or a supervisor is present. If a supervisor is present, the incident desk is staffed. The lab is open, but no technician is present. Therefore, the incident desk is staffed.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — disjunctive syllogism followed by modus ponens.
>
> Let L mean the lab is open, T a technician is present, S a supervisor is present, and D the incident desk is staffed. Numbered form: (1) L → (T ∨ S); (2) S → D; (3) L; (4) ¬T; conclusion: D. From (1) and (3), derive T ∨ S; with (4), disjunctive syllogism gives S; modus ponens with (2) gives D. The argument is valid.

#### A.3.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> If the grant is renewed, the lab hires an assistant; if it is not renewed, the lab reduces weekend hours. Hiring an assistant preserves weekday service, and reducing weekend hours preserves weekday service. The grant is either renewed or not renewed. Therefore, weekday service is preserved.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Valid — proof by cases (equivalently, constructive dilemma through the two cases).
>
> Let G mean the grant is renewed, H an assistant is hired, R weekend hours are reduced, and W weekday service is preserved. Numbered form: (1) G → H; (2) ¬G → R; (3) H → W; (4) R → W; (5) G ∨ ¬G; conclusion: W. In the G case, H and then W follow; in the ¬G case, R and then W follow. The cases are exhaustive, so the argument is valid by proof by cases (a constructive dilemma followed by either branch's implication to W).

#### A.3.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The report is accurate exactly when both sensors agree and their calibration is current. The sensors agree, and an automatic check completed, but the premises do not say that this check establishes current calibration. Therefore, the report is accurate.
> **Short response:** Write *valid* and the rule, or *invalid* and a countermodel.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Invalid — countermodel $R=\text{false}, A=\text{true}, C=\text{false}, K=\text{true}$.
>
> Let $R$ mean “the report is accurate,” $A$ mean “the sensors agree,” $C$ mean “their calibration is current,” and $K$ mean “an automatic check completed.” The argument is:
>
> 1. $R \leftrightarrow (A \land C)$
> 2. $A$
> 3. $K$
> 4. Therefore, $R$.
>
> The biconditional gives $(A \land C) \rightarrow R$, but the premises establish only $A$. Nothing connects $K$ to $C$. The word “check” may suggest current calibration in ordinary conversation, but formal evaluation may use only stated relationships.
>
> The argument is invalid. A countermodel is $R=false$, $A=true$, $C=false$, and $K=true$. In that model, $A \land C$ is false, so the biconditional $R \leftrightarrow (A \land C)$ is true; premises 2 and 3 are also true, while the conclusion is false.
>
> Add $C$ as a premise, or add $K \rightarrow C$. In the second repair, $K$ and $K \rightarrow C$ yield $C$; conjunction yields $A \land C$; then the sufficient direction of the biconditional yields $R$.

## B. Logical Reasoning I: Anatomy and Inference

> [!TIP]- Step-by-step selection method for Section B
> 1. Read the question stem first and name the task: conclusion, role, disagreement, must-follow, or parallel reasoning.
> 2. Reduce the passage to its logical skeleton. Separate what is stated from what is merely plausible.
> 3. Predict the kind of answer needed before reading the choices.
> 4. Eliminate choices that change the conclusion, reverse a relationship, overstate a quantifier, or match only the topic.
> 5. Recheck the surviving choice against the passage. For must-follow questions, try to make the choice false while keeping every fact true; for parallel questions, match the form rather than the subject matter.

> [!CHECK]- Minimum full-credit response and decisive checks
> For every B problem, the correct selected answer is the complete student response. The notes below explain the key for study and grading; students do not write them on the Core.
> - **B.1 Main conclusion:** The correct choice is the author's ultimate point, not evidence or a concession.
> - **B.2 Role in argument:** The correct choice accurately identifies how the cited claim supports, qualifies, or opposes another claim.
> - **B.3 Point at issue:** The correct choice states the exact proposition one speaker accepts and the other rejects, not merely the topic.
> - **B.4 Must follow:** The correct choice is forced by the stated facts without an added assumption.
> - **B.5 Parallel reasoning:** The correct choice matches the logical structure, including polarity and quantifier strength.

> - **(b)** If tea or coffee is available, a hot drink can be served. If a hot drink can be served, the station opens. Coffee is available, so the station opens.

> - **(d)** If the road is dry, travel is safe. If travel is safe, the route opens. The road is not dry, so the route does not open.
> - **(e)** If one witness agrees or a recording exists, the claim is reviewed. If reviewed, a report is filed. No witness agrees, so no report is filed.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> Both arguments have (P or Q) -> R, R -> S, and Q, therefore S. A confuses a conjunctive antecedent with a disjunctive one; C affirms a consequent; D and E deny antecedents.

#### B.5.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *The beacon flashes exactly when both the transmitter is active and the channel is clear. The beacon flashes. Therefore, the transmitter is active.*
>
> Which argument uses the same pattern of reasoning?
> - **(a)** The archive opens exactly when a curator is present and the alarm is disarmed. The archive is open, so a curator is present.
> - **(b)** If a curator is present and the alarm is disarmed, the archive opens. The archive is open, so a curator is present.
> - **(c)** The archive opens unless it is Sunday or the alarm is armed. It is open, so a curator is present.
> - **(d)** Only when a curator is present and the alarm is disarmed does the archive open. A curator is present, so the archive opens.
> - **(e)** The archive opens exactly when a curator is present or the alarm is disarmed. The archive is open, so both conditions hold.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> Both use P exactly when (Q and R), plus P, to conclude Q. B affirms the consequent of a one-way rule, C changes the condition, D establishes only half of a conjunctive necessary condition, and E changes conjunction to disjunction.

#### B.5.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *If the server is overloaded, requests slow down. Either the server is overloaded or the network is disconnected. Requests did not slow down. Therefore, the network is disconnected.*
>
> Which argument uses the same pattern of reasoning?
> - **(a)** If the path is icy, travel slows. Travel is slow or the path is closed. The path is not icy, so the path is closed.
> - **(b)** If the lock fails, the door opens. The lock fails or the alarm sounds. The door opens, so the alarm did not sound.
> - **(c)** If the lamp is on, the room is bright. The lamp is off or the curtains are closed. The room is not bright, so the curtains are closed.
> - **(d)** If the pump runs, water flows. Either the pump runs or the valve is closed. Water did not flow, so the valve is closed.
> - **(e)** If the form is late, a fee applies. Either no fee applies or the form is late. A fee applies, so the form was late.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Why D is correct:** The stimulus has the form
> $$P\rightarrow Q,\quad P\lor R,\quad \neg Q\quad\therefore\quad R.$$
> From $P\rightarrow Q$ and $\neg Q$, modus tollens gives $\neg P$. From $P\lor R$ and $\neg P$, disjunctive syllogism gives $R$. Choice D preserves this exact sequence: pump runs $\rightarrow$ water flows; pump runs or valve closed; water did not flow; therefore valve closed.
>
> A has “travel is slow or the path is closed,” not “icy or closed,” so its disjunction does not contain the antecedent. B affirms the consequent and then draws an unsupported conclusion about the alarm. C’s disjunction contains “lamp off,” the negation of the antecedent, rather than the antecedent itself. E again uses a differently structured disjunction and affirms the consequent.

## C. Logical Reasoning II: Assumptions and Evidence

> [!TIP]- Step-by-step selection method for Section C
> 1. Identify the conclusion and the evidence offered for it.
> 2. Write the missing link as a short question: *What must connect this evidence to that conclusion?*
> 3. Use the test required by the problem family: negate a necessary assumption, add a sufficient assumption, name the precise flaw, or ask whether new evidence changes the weak link.
> 4. Test each tempting choice against the actual inference. A relevant fact is not automatically a necessary assumption, a sufficient assumption, a strengthener, or a weakener.
> 5. Choose the answer that changes or describes the support relation most directly; do not substitute whether you agree with the conclusion.

> [!CHECK]- Minimum full-credit response and decisive checks
> For every C problem, the correct selected answer is the complete student response. The tests below explain how to identify that answer; they are not additional required writing.
> - **C.1 Necessary assumption:** Negating the correct choice breaks the stated reasoning.
> - **C.2 Sufficient assumption:** Adding the correct choice makes the conclusion follow; it need not be necessary.
> - **C.3 Flaw:** The correct choice identifies the precise inferential gap in the passage.
> - **C.4 Strengthen / C.5 Weaken:** The correct choice improves or damages the argument's actual support link, not merely the topic.

### C.1 Necessary Assumption

Select the assumption the argument requires. If it were false, the stated reasoning would fail.

#### C.1.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Team Delta keeps encrypted backups of its project files, and last month's recovery drill restored every file selected for the drill. The backup system can restore any file included in the most recently completed encrypted backup even if all team laptops are lost. Delta's manager therefore concludes that, if the laptops were lost tonight, every project file currently in use could be restored.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** Every project file currently in use is included in Delta's most recently completed encrypted backup.
> - **(b)** The recovery drill used files from more than one of Delta's projects.
> - **(c)** Encrypted backups cost less than replacing the team's laptops.
> - **(d)** No team elsewhere in the organization uses a different backup system.
> - **(e)** The files selected for the drill were more valuable than the files not selected.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> The restoration guarantee reaches only files in the most recently completed backup. Because the conclusion covers every file now in use and concerns a loss tonight, the reasoning requires each current file to be in that backup. Negating A leaves at least one current file outside the only stated restoration guarantee.

#### C.1.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *During the month after a museum installed color-coded directional signs, the proportion of visitors who asked staff for directions fell sharply. Attendance remained about the same, and the museum did not reduce the number of staffed information desks. The facilities director concludes that the signs, rather than some unrelated change, produced most of the decline in requests.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** Visitors who noticed the signs generally prefer reading them to speaking with museum staff.
> - **(b)** No change unrelated to the signs accounts for most of the decline in requests for directions.
> - **(c)** Each gallery used the same color for exits as every other gallery did.
> - **(d)** A majority of all visitors noticed at least one of the new signs.
> - **(e)** Reducing requests for directions allowed staff to complete other work.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Why B is required:** The conclusion is contrastive: the signs, **rather than some unrelated change**, produced most of the decline. Negate B: suppose an unrelated change did account for most of the decline. The director’s conclusion would then be false even though every stated premise could remain true. B therefore passes the negation test for a necessary assumption.
>
> A may help explain a mechanism but is not required; visitors could use signs even if they do not prefer them. C adds an irrelevant design detail. D is stronger than necessary: the signs need to affect enough of the people who would otherwise ask for directions, not necessarily a majority of all visitors. E is a possible consequence, not a required link in the causal inference.

#### C.1.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every purchase request approved by the university bears a valid budget officer's signature and appears in the procurement database. Rowan's request bears such a signature, although the database will not refresh until tomorrow. The department chair nevertheless concludes that Rowan's request has already been approved.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** Rowan submitted the request before the ordinary budget deadline.
> - **(b)** Budget officers sign substantially more approved requests than unapproved ones.
> - **(c)** If Rowan's request had not been approved, it would not bear a valid budget officer's signature.
> - **(d)** Rowan's request costs less than most requests already listed in the database.
> - **(e)** Every request signed this month will appear in the database after its next refresh.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> The stated rule makes a valid signature necessary for approval, but the chair reasons in the reverse direction for Rowan's request. C excludes the specific counterexample: Rowan's request has a valid signature but is not approved. Negating C allows exactly that situation, undermining the inference. No rule about every other signed request is required.

#### C.1.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *The library committee has funding for one of four submitted renovation plans. Plan K is the only plan that stays within the authorized budget and can be completed before the building reopens; another plan offers more study seats but misses the deadline. The committee should therefore adopt Plan K rather than postpone renovation.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** No submitted plan costs less than Plan K.
> - **(b)** The committee has previously approved a plan that offered fewer study seats than an alternative.
> - **(c)** Plan K contains every feature mentioned in the committee's original request.
> - **(d)** Neither postponing renovation nor selecting a plan that violates a stated constraint is preferable to adopting the sole plan that satisfies both constraints.
> - **(e)** The architects who prepared Plan K have completed an on-time library renovation before.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> The argument treats K's unique satisfaction of the budget and deadline as decisive against both postponement and the alternatives. If either postponement or knowingly violating a constraint were preferable to adopting the sole feasible plan, the stated comparison would not justify K. D rules out that defeat without requiring K to have every imaginable advantage.

#### C.1.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Before a software build can be released, an automated checker tests its privacy settings, dependency licenses, and accessibility labels. The checker reports warnings as well as blocking errors. It reported two style warnings for Build 18 but no blocking error. The release manager concludes that Build 18 satisfies every requirement that could prevent its release.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** Build 18 was checked after all of its style warnings had been reviewed.
> - **(b)** The checker reports every instance of inefficient code as a warning or an error.
> - **(c)** A build that may be released never benefits from additional testing.
> - **(d)** Most builds with only style warnings are eventually released.
> - **(e)** Build 18 cannot violate a release-blocking requirement without the checker reporting a blocking error.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> The absence of a flag establishes permission only if every disqualifying failure relevant to Build 18 would produce a flag. If an unflagged build could still violate another release requirement, the conclusion could be false.

#### C.1.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A writing course at North Campus introduced a schedule requiring two rounds of peer feedback before each major revision. Compared with earlier offerings taught by the same instructor, the course produced stronger final drafts without increasing grading time. The South Campus course uses the same assignments but meets in a different format. Its chair predicts that adopting the schedule will likewise improve its final drafts.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** No difference between the two courses makes the peer-feedback schedule materially less effective at South Campus.
> - **(b)** Students at both campuses prefer peer feedback to comments from an instructor.
> - **(c)** North Campus will continue using the schedule in every later offering.
> - **(d)** The rubric used to compare final drafts measures quality with complete objectivity.
> - **(e)** The courses enroll approximately the same number of students in each section.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> The prediction transfers a result from one course to another. It requires that there be no relevant difference—such as class structure or implementation—that defeats the transfer. Exact enrollment and identical preferences are stronger than the argument needs.

#### C.1.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *At a city-sponsored cycling workshop, 186 volunteers from the Lakeview neighborhood completed a transportation survey. Nearly all favored converting one traffic lane into a protected bicycle lane. The workshop drew residents from every precinct, so the planning office concludes that Lakeview residents overwhelmingly favor the conversion.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** The proposed bicycle lane costs less than every other transportation improvement under review.
> - **(b)** Workshop volunteers are sufficiently representative of Lakeview residents in their views about the proposed conversion.
> - **(c)** Each survey respondent lives in the precinct through which the proposed lane would pass.
> - **(d)** Residents who oppose protected lanes understand the difference between protected and painted lanes.
> - **(e)** The workshop attracted at least as many volunteers as the planning office expected.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Why B is required:** The evidence concerns 186 volunteers at a cycling workshop; the conclusion concerns Lakeview residents as a whole. The argument requires the sample to be sufficiently representative on the very attitude being estimated. Negate B: if workshop volunteers are strongly more favorable toward bicycle lanes than the wider neighborhood, then even participation from every precinct does not justify the population conclusion.
>
> A is irrelevant to the survey inference. C demands residence along the proposed route, which is unnecessary for a neighborhood-wide opinion estimate. D speculates about opponents’ knowledge. E concerns expected turnout, not representativeness. Geographic coverage alone does not eliminate self-selection bias.

#### C.1.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *At closing time, the cabinet key is normally returned to the reception desk, hung on a labeled hook, or secured in the supply drawer. Tonight it is not on the desk and not on the hook. The sign-out sheet shows that every employee who borrowed it has returned it. The supervisor concludes that the key must now be in the locked supply drawer.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** No key other than the cabinet key is stored in the supply drawer.
> - **(b)** Only the evening supervisor is currently permitted to open the supply drawer.
> - **(c)** Given that all borrowers returned the key, the desk, hook, and drawer exhaust its possible present locations.
> - **(d)** The cabinet key was found on the labeled hook at closing time yesterday.
> - **(e)** A spare that opens the same cabinet is stored in a different building.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> Eliminating the desk and hook establishes the drawer only if those two locations plus the drawer exhaust the current possibilities. If the key could be in a pocket or another room, the elimination is incomplete.

#### C.1.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A transit survey compared each participant's commute before and after a new express route opened. The average reported commute became twelve minutes shorter, and the median also fell. The transit director concludes that the express route shortened the commute of every surveyed rider who used it, not merely that it reduced commuting time overall.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** No participant stopped commuting before the follow-up survey was conducted.
> - **(b)** The express route makes fewer stops than every local route serving the same corridor.
> - **(c)** More than half of all riders who used the express route participated in both surveys.
> - **(d)** No surveyed user of the express route had an unchanged or longer commute after it opened.
> - **(e)** Surveyed riders generally care more about travel time than about the comfort of a bus.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> A lower average is compatible with some individuals receiving no benefit or even having longer commutes. The universal conclusion requires ruling out every such case. Sample size does not turn an average improvement into an improvement for each rider.

#### C.1.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *In a pilot, applicants completed a revised registration form several minutes faster than the current form, while employees found the two forms equally easy to process. The revised form would require a one-time software update, however, and the pilot did not test every unusual application. The office should nevertheless replace the current form with the revision.*
>
> Which assumption does the argument require?
> - **(a)** The current registration form has been in use long enough for applicants to recognize it.
> - **(b)** Applicants who participated in the pilot all complete forms at approximately the same speed.
> - **(c)** The revision achieves its time savings by asking fewer questions than the current form.
> - **(d)** The office could reverse the software update if the revised form proved unsatisfactory.
> - **(e)** The revision's time savings are not outweighed by omitted information, transition costs, or other decision-relevant losses.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> A single advantage justifies replacement only if relevant disadvantages do not outweigh it. The argument does not require identical completion speeds or fewer questions; it requires the net case for replacement not to be defeated by, for example, missing information or excessive transition costs.

### C.2 Sufficient Assumption

Select the statement that, if added to the stated premises, makes the conclusion follow logically.

#### C.2.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A sensor receives field certification if it passes both the sustained-load test and the high-temperature test, provided that its calibration record is current. Sensor R passed the sustained-load test, and the laboratory confirmed that its calibration record is current. The engineering team therefore expects Sensor R to receive field certification.*
>
> Which assumption is sufficient to justify the conclusion?
> - **(a)** Sensor R passed the high-temperature test.
> - **(b)** Every sensor that receives field certification has passed the sustained-load test.
> - **(c)** Sensor R was calibrated before another sensor that received certification.
> - **(d)** The high-temperature test takes less time to administer than the load test.
> - **(e)** No uncertified sensor with a current calibration record failed both tests.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> The stimulus already supplies the load result and current calibration. Adding A supplies the remaining test result, completing every condition in the stated sufficient rule. The other choices describe necessary conditions, comparisons, or a negated combination that does not establish R's heat-test result.

#### C.2.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *The incident log establishes that the synchronization failure began either in the network service or in the database service; failures originating in the application layer produce a different log signature. The network and database services later failed separate health checks. The investigator concludes that the database service originated this synchronization failure.*
>
> Which assumption is sufficient to justify the conclusion?
> - **(a)** The network and database services exchange messages during every synchronization.
> - **(b)** The network service did not originate this synchronization failure.
> - **(c)** The database service has originated a synchronization failure with the same visible symptom before.
> - **(d)** The network service recovered before the database service passed its health check.
> - **(e)** No user reported an application-layer error during the incident.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> The stated disjunction and the added denial of its network alternative yield the database alternative by disjunctive syllogism. The other statements do not eliminate the network service as the cause.

#### C.2.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every manuscript accepted for the journal receives a permanent archive number after copyediting, whereas submissions still under review retain only a temporary tracking number. Manuscript M now has a permanent archive number, although its public page is not yet visible. The managing editor concludes that M has been accepted.*
>
> Which assumption is sufficient to justify the conclusion?
> - **(a)** At least two editors recommended that Manuscript M be accepted.
> - **(b)** Any rejected manuscript that is revised receives a new temporary tracking number.
> - **(c)** A manuscript receives a permanent archive number only if it has been accepted for the journal.
> - **(d)** Manuscript M's permanent archive number was issued after copyediting began.
> - **(e)** Every accepted manuscript eventually becomes visible to journal subscribers.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> The added 'only' statement supplies the converse conditional: if a manuscript receives a permanent archive number, then it was accepted. Applying that conditional to M's archive number entails acceptance.

#### C.2.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *The town can fund either a footbridge or a fountain this year, but not both. The footbridge would provide the only safe pedestrian route across the stream during spring floods. The fountain would improve the central square and cost slightly less. The council should nevertheless fund the footbridge rather than the fountain.*
>
> Which assumption is sufficient to justify the conclusion?
> - **(a)** A majority of residents who use the central square favor building the fountain.
> - **(b)** Some residents who would cross the footbridge would also visit the central square.
> - **(c)** The stream is difficult but not impossible to cross without a bridge during summer.
> - **(d)** When exactly one of these projects can be funded, providing the safe spring crossing should take priority over the fountain's lower cost and benefit to the square.
> - **(e)** The footbridge and fountain would be completed during the same construction season.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Why D is sufficient:** The town can select exactly one project. The footbridge uniquely provides a safe spring crossing, while the fountain offers a lower-cost improvement to the square. D supplies the missing priority rule: in precisely that conflict, the safety benefit outranks the fountain’s advantages. Applying the rule to the stated facts deductively yields the recommendation to fund the footbridge.
>
> A supports the fountain. B and E do not rank the projects. C gives a fact about summer travel but does not establish how to decide the spring-safety tradeoff. A sufficient-assumption answer must close the whole logical gap, not merely make the preferred conclusion somewhat more attractive.

#### C.2.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every member of the regional planning council serves on at least one of its working groups. Some consultants also attend working-group meetings, but they do not vote. Niko serves on the accessibility working group and votes on its recommendations. The chair concludes that Niko is a member of the planning council.*
>
> Which assumption is sufficient to justify the conclusion?
> - **(a)** The council has more voting members than the accessibility working group does.
> - **(b)** Niko attends every meeting of the accessibility working group.
> - **(c)** No planning-council member votes in more than two working groups.
> - **(d)** Consultants who attend working-group meetings may speak but may not vote.
> - **(e)** Everyone who votes on recommendations of the accessibility working group is a planning-council member.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> The added universal statement directly places every accessibility-working-group member on the council. Since Niko serves on that group, council membership follows. The original premise alone runs in the opposite direction.

#### C.2.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *In the month after an office introduced a proofing checklist, the error rate per hundred printed pages fell by nearly half. The office printed more pages than usual, and the staff members who used the checklist were the same people who had prepared documents the prior month. The director concludes that using the checklist caused the reduction in the error rate.*
>
> Which assumption is sufficient to justify the conclusion?
> - **(a)** The decrease was caused by a change between the two months, and the checklist was the only change capable of lowering the printing-error rate.
> - **(b)** The office printed at least ten percent more pages in the later month.
> - **(c)** Every employee reported that the checklist was easy to remember and use.
> - **(d)** Most errors counted in either month were noticed before documents were mailed.
> - **(e)** The checklist contains every proofing step used by the office's most accurate employee.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> A supplies both links needed for a sufficient causal explanation: some change caused the observed decrease, and only the checklist could be that change. Therefore the checklist caused the decrease. The first clause excludes a decline due solely to random fluctuation; the second excludes a rival change such as easier documents. A mere before-and-after comparison would not establish either link.

#### C.2.08

#### C.2.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement

### C.3 Diagnose the Flaw

Select the choice that most precisely describes the error in the argument's reasoning.

#### C.3.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Attendance at the courtyard concert series rose by one third during the summer after organizers added more benches. Ticket prices and the number of performances did not change, and surveys say that patrons value having a place to sit. The organizers conclude that the added benches caused the attendance increase.*
>
> Which choice most accurately describes the flaw?
> - **(a)** It infers that the benches caused the increase without excluding changes in performers, publicity, or other factors that could account for it.
> - **(b)** It assumes that every additional attendee sat on one of the new benches rather than standing.
> - **(c)** It overlooks the possibility that a few of the old and new benches remained empty at some performances.
> - **(d)** It shifts from the total number of tickets sold to the average attendance at each performance.
> - **(e)** It treats patrons' stated preference for seating as evidence that no patron values any other feature of the series.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> **Why A is correct:** The argument observes a before/after association and attributes the entire change to the benches. Holding ticket price and number of performances fixed removes two alternatives, and the survey provides a plausible mechanism, but other simultaneous changes—performers, publicity, weather, scheduling, or population—could still explain the attendance increase. The design lacks a comparison group or adequate control for confounders.
>
> B is unnecessarily strong; benches could increase attendance even if not every new attendee sits. C is compatible with a causal effect. D describes a shift the passage does not make. E misstates the inference: the organizers need not assume patrons value nothing else. A identifies the exact causal gap without exaggerating it.

#### C.3.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every package that passes the full customs inspection receives a green seal before entering the warehouse. Packages cleared through a separate diplomatic procedure may also enter, and the record does not say which seals that procedure uses. Package Q is in the warehouse and bears a green seal. The manager concludes that Q passed the full customs inspection.*
>
> Which choice most accurately describes the flaw?
> - **(a)** It concludes that an inspected package can never lose or replace its green seal after entering the warehouse.
> - **(b)** It treats a green seal, which is guaranteed by full inspection, as though the seal could be obtained only through full inspection.
> - **(c)** It assumes that the diplomatic procedure always uses a seal whose color differs from green.
> - **(d)** It infers a universal rule governing all warehouse packages from the history of Package Q alone.
> - **(e)** It presumes that any customs inspection of Q occurred more recently than its diplomatic clearance.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B

> - **(b)** Residents working away from home during those hours were rarely reached, and a random sample of that group mostly opposes expansion.
> - **(c)** Existing evening buses stop at the same time each night in all districts covered by the survey.
> - **(d)** Respondents who favored and opposed expansion both supplied several different reasons for their answers.
> - **(e)** The proposed expansion would add weekend as well as weekday service, a detail stated before residents answered.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Why B most weakens:** Reaching every district does not make the sample
> representative if the survey is conducted when many working residents are
> away. B both identifies the systematically missed group and supplies evidence
> that its views differ: a random sample of that group mostly opposes expansion.
> The observed respondent majority may therefore be an artifact of when the
> calls were made rather than the view of most adults.
>
> A concerns an additional survey question. C describes the existing schedule.
> D shows varied reasoning on both sides. E says respondents were informed about
> the proposal. None of those choices undermines the sample-to-population
> generalization as directly as B.

#### C.5.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Many students use a campus learning center to print readings that are difficult to view on a phone. The center currently provides a modest free-printing allowance. Its director proposes unlimited free printing, arguing that this would make course materials easier for students to access overall.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** Some students understand long readings better on paper than on the screens they own.
> - **(b)** The printers can use both sides of a page, although students must select that setting themselves.
> - **(c)** Unlimited printing would consume the funds used for evening hours, when many students rely on the center's computers to access materials.
> - **(d)** Several courses assign readings with diagrams that are difficult to interpret on a small screen.
> - **(e)** One printer was replaced last year, and the remaining printers are somewhat older.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> **Why C most weakens:** The conclusion concerns access to course materials
> **overall**, not printing alone. C says unlimited printing would consume the
> funds for evening hours, when many students need the center's computers to
> reach those materials at all. The policy could therefore expand paper access
> for one group while reducing digital access for another. That tradeoff breaks
> the argument's move from “more free printing” to “better overall access.”
>
> A and D explain why printing may help and thus strengthen the proposal. B is
> an efficiency feature, and E concerns equipment age without showing a loss of
> access. C is the only choice that supplies a substantial countervailing harm
> to the exact goal named in the conclusion.

#### C.5.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *After a quiet-room policy was introduced at a branch office, employees completed independent record reviews more quickly with no loss of accuracy. Management plans to impose the same policy at headquarters, predicting a similar productivity gain. Headquarters has ten times as many employees, but its workspace has comparable acoustics.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** The branch and headquarters use the same payroll system and observe the same paid holidays.
> - **(b)** Some headquarters employees have completed independent record reviews while visiting the branch.
> - **(c)** The branch building is older, although measurements show that its rooms have comparable acoustics.
> - **(d)** Most headquarters tasks require frequent spoken coordination, whereas nearly all branch tasks are performed independently.
> - **(e)** Headquarters contains more rooms, but more employees on average share each room.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Why D most weakens:** The prediction transfers a successful branch policy
> to headquarters. D identifies a difference that is directly relevant to the
> policy's effect: branch work is largely independent, whereas headquarters
> work requires frequent spoken coordination. A quiet-room policy may speed
> isolated record review but obstruct the collaboration needed for most
> headquarters tasks. Comparable acoustics do not repair that task difference.
>
> A is operationally irrelevant. B shows limited cross-site experience but not
> that the policy works for headquarters work. C repeats an already controlled
> acoustic comparison. E describes crowding but does not say whether quiet
> helps or hurts productivity. D most directly blocks the analogy.

#### C.5.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every application reviewed by the compliance office receives a blue digital stamp that includes a date. Application T bears a current blue stamp and appears in the same portal used by the compliance office. The department therefore concludes that the compliance office reviewed T.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** Application T contains fewer pages than most applications reviewed this month.
> - **(b)** The compliance office ordinarily reviews applications in the order in which the portal receives them.
> - **(c)** Some stamped applications require revisions even after the compliance review is complete.
> - **(d)** The date in T's blue stamp matches the date on which it was uploaded to the portal.
> - **(e)** The portal automatically gives every uploaded application the same dated blue stamp before any compliance review occurs.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> **Why E most weakens:** The original rule is review $\rightarrow$ dated blue stamp. The conclusion reverses it: dated blue stamp $\rightarrow$ review. E provides a concrete alternative cause of the observed stamp—the portal applies it automatically before review—so T could have exactly the observed features without ever being reviewed.
>
> A concerns length, B concerns review order, and C concerns what happens after a completed review; none breaks the stamp-review connection as strongly. D merely aligns two dates and is fully compatible with review. E attacks the argument’s only evidence for its conclusion.

#### C.5.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Attendance at the town's outdoor film night increased in each of the last three weeks, even though the films represented different genres and the ticket price stayed constant. Organizers therefore predict another increase next week, when the film will again be shown in the central park.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** Next week's screening conflicts with the town's largest annual festival, which usually attracts much of the film series' audience.
> - **(b)** Each film begins shortly after sunset, which will occur earlier next week than it did at the last screening.
> - **(c)** Some regular attendees bring folding chairs while others use seating provided by the organizers.
> - **(d)** The most recent film was longer than the one shown the week before, despite attracting more people.
> - **(e)** Organizers list every screening on the same community calendar used during the recent increases.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> **Why A most weakens:** The forecast extrapolates three recent increases into
> next week. A introduces a new condition unique to the forecast period: the
> town's largest annual festival competes for much of the same audience. Even
> if the upward trend was genuine, the festival supplies a concrete reason it
> may reverse next week, so the past three weeks are no longer closely
> comparable to the predicted week.
>
> B is a small timing change with no stated adverse effect. C concerns seating
> preferences, D is compatible with the upward trend, and E preserves the same
> publicity conditions. A is the only choice that disrupts the continuity on
> which the forecast depends.

#### C.5.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *In a laboratory drop trial, every handheld device that remained operational had a metal case. Several metal-cased devices nevertheless failed, and the tested nonmetal devices used older polymer designs. The researcher concludes that a metal case is necessary for any handheld device to survive a fall of that height.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** Several metal-cased devices that remained operational were badly scratched during the trial.
> - **(b)** In a repeat at the same height, a device with a new nonmetal polymer case survived undamaged and operational.
> - **(c)** The laboratory recorded the weight and dimensions of each device before conducting the trial.
> - **(d)** The metal cases differed in thickness, and some enclosed older devices than others.
> - **(e)** Many handheld devices are unlikely ever to be dropped from the height used in the trial.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Why B most weakens:** Calling metal **necessary** means that no device can
> survive the fall without it: survival $\rightarrow$ metal case. B supplies a
> device that survived the same-height test while using a nonmetal case. That is
> a direct counterexample to the claimed necessity, and the newer polymer design
> also addresses the passage's warning that the original nonmetal cases were
> old.
>
> A concerns cosmetic damage to devices that still survived. C describes data
> collection. D varies the metal group without producing a nonmetal survivor.
> E questions real-world relevance but not the laboratory necessity claim. B
> attacks the universal conclusion itself.

#### C.5.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *In an optional application survey, users who enabled night-display mode reported better sleep during the following month than users who left the ordinary display enabled. Both groups used the application for similar amounts of time. The developer concludes that enabling night mode improved the users' sleep.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** Night-display mode changes both screen color and brightness rather than changing color alone.
> - **(b)** Some users enable night mode before sunset and continue using it throughout the evening.
> - **(c)** Before choosing a display mode, the users who later selected night mode already reported substantially better sleep.
> - **(d)** The application includes a daytime mode that automatically adjusts brightness in strong sunlight.
> - **(e)** Users may disable night mode at any time without deleting their other application settings.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> **Why C most weakens:** The groups chose their own display modes rather than
> being randomly assigned. C shows that the future night-mode group already
> slept substantially better before making that choice. The later difference
> can therefore be explained by a preexisting group difference or self-selection
> rather than an improvement caused by night mode. Similar application-use time
> does not control baseline sleep.
>
> A describes what the mode changes but does not show that it fails. B and E
> concern how the feature is used or disabled. D describes an unrelated daytime
> feature. C directly undermines the causal comparison by showing that the
> groups were unequal before treatment.

#### C.5.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every volunteer recruited from an advanced puzzle club solved a scheduling puzzle in under five minutes. Volunteers ranged widely in age, and they had not seen that exact puzzle before. The test designer concludes that most adults in the city could also solve it within five minutes.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** The puzzle contains both personal names and appointment times, as do many ordinary calendars.
> - **(b)** Some club volunteers finished considerably faster than the five-minute limit, while others used nearly all of it.
> - **(c)** The city contains far more adults than the advanced puzzle club has members.
> - **(d)** Admission to the club requires passing a timed test containing puzzles closely resembling the one used in the study.
> - **(e)** The volunteers used pencils and were allowed to make notes while solving the puzzle.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Why D most weakens:** The conclusion generalizes from advanced-puzzle-club
> volunteers to most city adults. D says club admission itself requires fast
> success on closely similar puzzles. The sample is therefore selected for the
> exact ability being measured, making its age diversity and unfamiliarity with
> this particular puzzle largely irrelevant. Performance by this group gives
> little evidence about typical adults.
>
> A notes superficial realism, B describes variation within the successful
> group, C merely compares population sizes, and E gives an ordinary testing
> condition. D identifies the decisive selection bias.

#### C.5.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *An application form contains several fields that first-time applicants often misunderstand. The agency proposes requiring every applicant to open an online tutorial before the submit button becomes active. Because the tutorial correctly explains every field, the agency concludes that this requirement will ensure that applicants complete the form correctly.*
>
> Which statement, if true, most weakens the argument?
> - **(a)** The tutorial contains examples for the fields that applicants most often misunderstand.
> - **(b)** Applicants may open the tutorial on a phone or computer before returning to the same form.
> - **(c)** Staff can return incorrectly completed forms for revision after the application deadline.
> - **(d)** The tutorial and form use identical labels for every corresponding field.
> - **(e)** Applicants may close the tutorial immediately without reading it and then activate the submit button as usual.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> **Why E most weakens:** The agency moves from “the tutorial is accurate” and “applicants must open it” to “applicants will complete the form correctly.” E breaks the behavioral link: opening need not involve reading, understanding, or following the tutorial. If applicants can close it immediately, the requirement may change nothing relevant to their answers.
>
> A and D improve the tutorial and therefore tend to strengthen the plan. B describes device flexibility without undermining learning. C concerns correction after submission and does not show that the requirement fails to improve initial accuracy. E directly shows why the proposed mechanism does not ensure the promised result.

## D. Logical Reasoning III: Principles and Explanations

> [!TIP]- Step-by-step selection method for Section D
> 1. Translate the argument or principle into a neutral pattern before reading the choices.
> 2. List the conditions that must be matched, the two facts that must be reconciled, or the invalid move that must be repeated.
> 3. Test every part of a candidate answer; one missing condition or reversed relationship is enough to reject it.
> 4. Prefer the narrow answer that performs the required logical job. Do not choose a broad moral, a topical similarity, or a claim that merely could be true.
> 5. Read the passage once more with the selected answer inserted and verify that the gap, discrepancy, or structural match is actually resolved.

> [!CHECK]- Minimum full-credit response and decisive checks
> For every D problem, the correct selected answer is the complete student response. The checks below explain the answer key; students do not write them on the Core.
> - **D.1 Principle support:** Every operative condition of the correct principle maps to a stated fact.
> - **D.2 Resolve discrepancy:** The correct choice permits both apparently conflicting observations to be true.
> - **D.3 Parallel flaw:** The correct choice contains the same invalid move as the stimulus.
> - **D.4 Method of reasoning:** The correct choice describes what the author actually does, not a method the author could have used.
> - **D.5 Complete the argument:** The correct choice closes the exact gap without adding an unnecessarily stronger claim.

### D.1 Principle Support

Select the principle that most strongly supports the stated judgment. No written justification is required.

#### D.1.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A public library can afford to preserve only one of two collections this year. One contains unique local oral-history recordings used by relatively few patrons; the other contains popular novels that remain available from many other libraries. The director chooses the recordings.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the director's decision?
> - **(a)** When preservation resources are scarce, irreplaceable material should take priority over material readily obtainable elsewhere.
> - **(b)** A library should devote equal resources to every category of material it owns.
> - **(c)** The collection used by the greatest number of patrons should always be preserved first.
> - **(d)** Libraries should preserve only material created in their own region.
> - **(e)** Older material should be preserved before newer material, regardless of its availability.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> Choice A supplies the needed priority rule: scarcity favors the unique recordings because the novels can be replaced from other sources. B requires equal treatment rather than a choice, C points toward the novels, and D and E introduce criteria the director did not use.

#### D.1.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A software cooperative promised users that it would promptly disclose defects that could seriously compromise their data. Engineers discover such a defect just before a major product demonstration. The cooperative's manager says it should disclose the defect now even though doing so may embarrass the organization.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the manager's judgment?
> - **(a)** An organization should delay any announcement that might be misunderstood by the public.
> - **(b)** An organization should honor a relevant public commitment even when honoring it creates a foreseeable reputational cost.
> - **(c)** Technical information should be released only after every known defect has been repaired.
> - **(d)** Organizations should disclose information only when disclosure improves sales.
> - **(e)** A promise may be ignored whenever circumstances make compliance inconvenient.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Why B is correct:** The relevant facts match B condition by condition: the cooperative publicly promised prompt disclosure; the discovered defect could seriously compromise user data; and immediate disclosure carries a foreseeable reputational cost because of the demonstration. B says that such a cost does not cancel the obligation to honor the commitment, so it directly supports disclosure now.
>
> A and C introduce delay conditions absent from the promise. D makes sales rather than the commitment decisive. E would permit breaking a promise whenever compliance is inconvenient—the exact reasoning the manager rejects. B is neither broader nor narrower than the judgment it is meant to justify.

#### D.1.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A scholarship application arrived six hours late because the submission website, which the scholarship committee alone operated, was unavailable during the final day. The application was otherwise complete. The committee chair recommends accepting it for review.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the chair's recommendation?
> - **(a)** Deadlines should be extended whenever an applicant says completion was difficult.
> - **(b)** A late application should be accepted whenever the applicant is highly qualified.
> - **(c)** A decision maker should not penalize a person for missing a procedural requirement when the decision maker's own failure prevented timely compliance.
> - **(d)** Online applications should never have fixed deadlines.
> - **(e)** All applicants should receive an automatic one-day extension.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> C connects the committee's own website failure to a justified exception from its deadline. The recommendation does not depend on the applicant's qualifications or on opposition to deadlines generally, so B and D do not support it; A and E are much broader than the facts warrant.

#### D.1.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Members of a community garden share both individual plots and common paths. A member repeatedly refuses scheduled path maintenance but asks for first choice of the newly available plots. The coordinators decide that members who completed their shared maintenance should choose first.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the coordinators' decision?
> - **(a)** Community resources should always be distributed by lottery.
> - **(b)** Anyone who requests a benefit first should receive it first.
> - **(c)** A volunteer who misses one task should permanently lose all community benefits.
> - **(d)** When a cooperative benefit is scarce, priority may be given to members who have fulfilled the responsibilities that sustain the shared project.
> - **(e)** Only coordinators should be eligible for desirable plots.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> D links priority for a scarce cooperative benefit to performance of the duties that maintain the garden, matching both relevant facts. A and B prescribe unrelated allocation rules, while C is a permanent and disproportionate penalty and E rests on status rather than contribution.

#### D.1.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *An instructor's project directions used the word 'source' in two incompatible ways. Several students followed the reasonable interpretation discussed in class and lost points under the other interpretation. The instructor decides to restore those points.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the instructor's decision?
> - **(a)** Every student who requests a higher mark should receive one.
> - **(b)** Assignments containing any ambiguity should be removed from a course.
> - **(c)** Students should receive credit for effort even when their work does not address an assignment.
> - **(d)** An evaluator should never revise a score after returning it.
> - **(e)** When an evaluator's materially ambiguous instructions reasonably produce a response, the evaluator should not penalize that response for choosing the unintended interpretation.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> E narrowly covers the case: the ambiguity was the instructor's, it was material, and the students' interpretation was reasonable. The other choices either require indiscriminate changes, remove the task entirely, substitute effort for correctness, or forbid justified correction.

#### D.1.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A transit agency is considering ending a late-evening route because it carries few riders. Those riders are night-shift workers, and no other public route serves the area after their shifts. The agency decides to keep the route while seeking a less expensive vehicle for it.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the agency's decision?
> - **(a)** A public service may deserve continued support despite low use when it provides essential access that its users cannot obtain through a reasonable alternative.
> - **(b)** Every transit route should use the largest vehicle in the agency's fleet.
> - **(c)** Routes with fewer riders should receive more funding than all other routes.
> - **(d)** No public service should ever be changed once people rely on it.
> - **(e)** Transit agencies should operate only routes that generate a profit.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> A explains why low ridership does not settle the decision: the route provides essential transportation with no reasonable substitute. Seeking a smaller vehicle is consistent with preserving access. B and C concern resource levels, D forbids even sensible modifications, and E supports cancellation.

#### D.1.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A laboratory paper lists only researchers with faculty or student titles as authors. A technician designed a calibration procedure without which the reported measurements would not have been reliable. The lab director says the technician should also be credited as an author.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the director's judgment?
> - **(a)** Every employee of a laboratory should be an author of every paper it produces.
> - **(b)** Credit for a result should track a person's substantive intellectual contribution rather than merely the person's job title.
> - **(c)** Only the person who writes the final draft should receive authorship credit.
> - **(d)** Technical work should be credited only when it takes more time than the theoretical work.
> - **(e)** Authorship should be assigned in order of seniority.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Why B is correct:** The technician designed a calibration procedure necessary to the reliability of the reported measurements. That is a substantive intellectual contribution to the result. B makes substantive contribution—not faculty/student status—the basis for credit and therefore directly supports adding the technician as an author.
>
> A is overinclusive because employment alone is not authorship. C is too narrow because authorship can reflect design, analysis, or methodology as well as drafting. D substitutes hours worked for intellectual contribution, and E substitutes hierarchy for contribution. Only B connects the stated fact to the director’s judgment.

#### D.1.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A majority of the student council favors reserving all tutoring appointments for students in honors programs. The council's charter guarantees equal eligibility for academic support to all enrolled students. The presiding officer rules the proposal out of order.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the officer's ruling?
> - **(a)** A proposal supported by a majority must always receive a vote.
> - **(b)** Honors students should receive every academic resource they request.
> - **(c)** A governing body should not adopt a majority preference that conflicts with a binding guarantee the body is obligated to follow.
> - **(d)** Rules governing student organizations should never be amended.
> - **(e)** Tutoring appointments should be assigned only by instructors.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> C establishes that the charter's binding equal-eligibility guarantee limits what a majority may enact, which justifies ruling the proposal out of order. A ignores that limit; B and E introduce unsupported allocation rules; D concerns amendment rather than compliance with the current charter.

#### D.1.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A newspaper placed an unsupported accusation on its front page. After checking the records, the editors learn that the accusation was false. They decide that the correction should appear with prominence comparable to the original claim rather than in a small notice at the back.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the editors' decision?
> - **(a)** Corrections should be published only when the person criticized requests one.
> - **(b)** A publication should never report a disputed allegation.
> - **(c)** Any correction is adequate as long as it is eventually printed.
> - **(d)** A publication responsible for a consequential false impression should make its correction reasonably capable of reaching the audience exposed to that impression.
> - **(e)** Front pages should contain only positive news.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> D ties the visibility of the remedy to the audience and consequence of the publication's own error, supporting comparable prominence. A makes correction depend on a request, B and E impose unrelated categorical rules, and C ignores whether the correction can repair the impression.

#### D.1.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A debate moderator personally considers the environmental topic more important than the budget topic. Nevertheless, the moderator gives each side the same announced speaking time on both topics. One speaker asks for extra time because the environmental topic matters more to the moderator, but the request is denied.*
>
> Which principle most strongly supports the denial?
> - **(a)** Debates should avoid subjects on which moderators have opinions.
> - **(b)** More important topics should always receive unlimited discussion time.
> - **(c)** A moderator should allow the stronger speaker more time.
> - **(d)** Announced procedures may be changed whenever one participant requests it.
> - **(e)** A neutral administrator should apply announced procedural limits consistently rather than adjust them according to a personal view of a topic's importance.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> E makes consistent administration, not the moderator's personal priorities, the controlling consideration. The equal announced limits therefore support denial. A is unnecessary, B and C undermine neutrality, and D would make the procedure depend on requests rather than consistent treatment.

### D.2 Resolve the Discrepancy

Select the fact that best allows both reported observations to be true. No written justification is required.

#### D.2.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A campus cafe completed fewer customer transactions this September than last September, yet revenue from food and drink was substantially higher. Individual menu-item prices and the number of days the cafe was open were unchanged. The cafe counted an order containing several items as one transaction in both years.*
>
> Which fact best resolves the apparent discrepancy?
> - **(a)** This September, many more customers bought multi-item meals, so the average number of items in each transaction increased sharply.
> - **(b)** The same menu board was displayed, but customers stood closer to it while waiting this September.
> - **(c)** Several experienced employees worked in both years and processed transactions somewhat faster this September.
> - **(d)** The cafe's rent rose before this September, though rent is not included in food-and-drink revenue.
> - **(e)** A larger share of customers paid by card, but the cafe received the same amount for an item regardless of payment method.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> Transactions count purchases, whereas revenue reflects how much is sold in them. If many more transactions contained multi-item bundles, average revenue per transaction could rise enough for total revenue to increase despite fewer transactions. The other facts do not connect transaction count to revenue per transaction.

#### D.2.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *During a month of road construction, a campus shuttle's temporary route was two kilometers longer than its regular route, yet its average end-to-end trip was eight minutes shorter. The shuttle, driver schedule, number of passenger stops, and method for measuring trip time did not change.*
>
> Which fact best resolves the apparent discrepancy?
> - **(a)** The shuttle was repainted shortly before construction, making its route number easier to see from a distance.
> - **(b)** The temporary route used a bus-only lane and bypassed the two intersections responsible for most delays on the regular route.
> - **(c)** Students disliked construction noise near one stop, although the number of passengers boarding there was unchanged.
> - **(d)** The regular route's two-kilometer distance advantage was greatest near the middle of the trip.
> - **(e)** The assigned drivers received new uniforms but retained the same driving schedules and speed rules.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> Distance and travel time need not move together. The bus-only lane can eliminate long intersection delays, more than offsetting the added distance. D merely repeats the discrepancy, while the remaining facts offer no mechanism affecting travel time.

#### D.2.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A department replaced an introductory online module with one containing longer and more demanding exercises. The deadline, enrollment rules, and credit for completion remained unchanged. Even so, a larger percentage of enrolled students completed the new module by the deadline.*
>
> Which fact best resolves the apparent discrepancy?
> - **(a)** The new module used a higher-contrast background color that students described as more comfortable to view.
> - **(b)** Both modules included diagrams, although the new module's diagrams contained more labels.
> - **(c)** Unlike the old module, the new one saved each response automatically and allowed interrupted students to resume without repeating work.
> - **(d)** Before the deadline, the department reminded students that the new exercises were more demanding.
> - **(e)** The deadline fell on the same weekday, but the calendar date differed between the two terms.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> More demanding content can coexist with higher completion when an independent obstacle is removed. Automatic saving prevents interrupted students from losing work and can raise completion enough to outweigh increased difficulty. The other choices do not explain the changed completion rate.

#### D.2.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Electronic gate counts show that fewer people entered the library this winter than last winter, yet the catalog recorded more new loans of physical books. Loan periods, renewal rules, collection size, and the definition of a new loan were unchanged.*
>
> Which fact best resolves the apparent discrepancy?
> - **(a)** The library purchased reading-room chairs, but furniture deliveries were not counted by the electronic gates.
> - **(b)** Many visitors who entered used wireless service without borrowing a physical book.
> - **(c)** This winter was colder than the preceding autumn, though about as cold as last winter.
> - **(d)** The library introduced outdoor lockers where patrons could initiate physical-book loans without entering the building.
> - **(e)** The books borrowed this winter varied more in weight, but every book still counted as one loan.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Why D resolves the discrepancy:** Gate counts measure entries into the building, while the catalog measures new physical-book loans. Outdoor lockers uncouple those measures: a patron can initiate a loan and collect the book without passing through the gate. If enough patrons use the lockers, building entries can fall while loans rise, with all of the stated counting rules unchanged.
>
> A concerns nonpatron deliveries. B explains why some entrants do not borrow but gives no reason for more loans with fewer entrants. C does not distinguish the two winters. E changes book weight, which is irrelevant when each loan counts once. D alone supplies a mechanism that pushes the measures in the reported directions.

#### D.2.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A greenhouse's meter recorded less total water use this spring than last spring, but its tomato plants produced more new growth. The tomato varieties, planting density, growing period, and sunlight were comparable, and staff used the same method to measure both water and growth.*
>
> Which fact best resolves the apparent discrepancy?
> - **(a)** The same water meter remained near the entrance and measured water before it reached individual plants.
> - **(b)** Tomatoes were not the only plants, but their share of greenhouse floor space was unchanged.
> - **(c)** Staff measured growth weekly rather than relying only on one measurement at the end of spring.
> - **(d)** The tomatoes were harvested after the measurement period ended in both years.
> - **(e)** This spring drip lines delivered water directly to roots instead of losing much of it by spraying floors and paths.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E

### D.3 Parallel Flaw

Select the argument whose reasoning contains the same flaw as the stimulus. No written justification is required.

#### D.3.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *If every archive box has been cataloged and the search index has finished refreshing, every record can be found through the public search page. Staff can now find every record through that page. Therefore, every box has been cataloged and the index has finished refreshing.*
>
> Which argument contains the same flaw?
> - **(a)** If a classroom is occupied and its projector is active, the status panel glows. The panel glows, so the room is occupied and its projector is active.
> - **(b)** If a badge is valid and the gate is online, the badge opens the gate. This badge is valid and the gate is online, so it opens the gate.
> - **(c)** If the window is open or the smoke sensor activates, the alarm sounds. The alarm did not sound, so neither condition occurred.
> - **(d)** The parcel is upstairs or downstairs but not both. It is not upstairs, so it is downstairs.
> - **(e)** Every scanned and signed form is indexed. This form was scanned and signed, so it is indexed.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> **Why A is correct:** The stimulus has the invalid form
> $$(P \land Q)\rightarrow R,\quad R\quad\therefore\quad P\land Q.$$
> It affirms the consequent: public searchability may result from some condition other than both cataloging and a refreshed index. Choice A repeats the form exactly: occupied-and-projector-active $\rightarrow$ glowing panel; glowing panel; therefore both antecedent conditions.
>
> B and E validly apply modus ponens. C validly reasons by contraposition from $(W\lor S)\rightarrow A$ and $\neg A$ to $\neg(W\lor S)$, hence neither condition. D validly uses disjunctive syllogism with an exclusive disjunction. The shared flaw is not merely “assuming too much”; it is reversing a conditional with a conjunctive antecedent.

> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement

> - **(b)** If the oven is preheated or the dough rests overnight, the bread will rise. The oven was not preheated, so the bread will not rise.
> - **(c)** If a file is encrypted or stored remotely, it requires a key. This file is encrypted, so it requires a key.
> - **(d)** The meeting is virtual or in Room 8 but not both. It is not virtual, so it is in Room 8.
> - **(e)** Every copper or silver token conducts electricity. This token does not conduct electricity, so it is neither copper nor silver.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> The stated sufficient condition has two alternative ways to trigger hiring. Rejecting grant renewal leaves reserve funding open, so the conclusion denies the consequence after eliminating only one disjunct. B repeats that error because overnight resting remains an open route to rising. A and E use contraposition; C and D validly apply their premises.

> The stimulus wrongly infers that membership in one group guarantees membership in another merely because both groups lie within a third: novels and manuals may both have complex plots without any novel being a manual. > [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> The premises require at least one of the two roads for emergency access and remove the north road from consideration. Therefore maintaining that access requires the south road to remain open. The other choices add claims about inspections, timing, past closure, or need that the premises do not establish.

#### D.5.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A survey about dining-hall satisfaction was sent only to students who had filed a dining complaint during the previous month. Nearly all recipients responded, and most respondents were dissatisfied. Since the selection method made recipients more likely than students generally to be dissatisfied, \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** each dissatisfied respondent must have filed more than one complaint during the previous month
> - **(b)** students who did not receive the survey are more likely than not to be satisfied
> - **(c)** the dining hall may disregard the complaints because the survey sample was biased
> - **(d)** the survey alone does not justify concluding that most students overall are dissatisfied
> - **(e)** a still larger survey restricted to complaint filers would probably show majority satisfaction

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> The selection rule produces a sample predisposed toward dissatisfaction, so its majority cannot by itself support a conclusion about all students. D states that appropriately limited conclusion. The premises do not establish how many complaints each respondent filed, the views of all nonrecipients, or what another biased survey would find.

#### D.5.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *An archive's stated first priority is preventing the permanent loss of records that exist nowhere else; increasing access is important but secondary. Of this year's proposed projects, only digitizing the deteriorating field notebooks would preserve records with no duplicate. The other projects improve access to stable duplicated material. Therefore, \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** every record lacking a digital copy should be digitized before any access project begins
> - **(b)** the archive should stop accepting new records until the field notebooks are preserved
> - **(c)** the deteriorating field notebooks must be the archive's most frequently consulted records
> - **(d)** a preservation project is worthwhile only if it produces digital files for public access
> - **(e)** digitizing the field notebooks should receive priority among this year's proposed projects

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> **Why E completes the argument:** The archive’s first priority is preventing permanent loss of unique records. Among this year’s proposals, only digitizing the deteriorating notebooks serves that first priority; the others serve the explicitly secondary goal of access. Therefore the notebooks project should rank first among the proposals, exactly as E states.
>
> A expands a comparative decision about this year’s projects into an unlimited rule about every undigitized record. B concerns new acquisitions. C confuses preservation priority with frequency of use. D says preservation has value only when it also produces public access, contrary to the stated ordering of priorities.

#### D.5.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A geology field trip may proceed at the selected research site only if the site issues a written access permit and the department supplies a trained driver. The department has assigned a driver, but the site has definitively declined the permit and that requirement cannot be waived. Consequently, \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** the field trip may not proceed at the selected site as planned
> - **(b)** the site will never issue a permit for a field trip on any later date
> - **(c)** the written-permit requirement is unfair despite the assigned driver
> - **(d)** students may visit the selected site without an instructor because a driver is available
> - **(e)** every alternative site suitable for the trip also requires a written permit

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> A written permit is a necessary condition for this trip, and the condition is unmet with no waiver available. Therefore the trip cannot proceed as planned. The remaining choices make broad predictions or recommendations and evaluations absent from the premises.

#### D.5.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Every recipient of the presentation award both delivered a talk during the conference and was present for the final session. Maya delivered a talk and was present on the first two days, but she was not present for the final session. It follows that \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** Maya did not deliver a talk during the conference
> - **(b)** Maya did not receive the presentation award
> - **(c)** everyone who presented and attended the final session received the award
> - **(d)** Maya was absent from every conference session after giving her talk
> - **(e)** the presentation award was not announced during the final session

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> The premise says award recipient implies present at the final session. Since Maya was not present, contraposition entails that she was not an award recipient, which is B. The premise does not state who presented, whether presence was sufficient, or anything about other sessions or award timing.

#### D.5.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A conference room is eligible to be chosen by the committee if and only if it is both within budget and wheelchair accessible. Room X is accessible and has better projection equipment but exceeds the budget. Room Y has ordinary equipment, is accessible, and is within budget. These are the only available rooms. Thus, \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** Room X should be chosen because its equipment outweighs the budget condition
> - **(b)** the committee cannot choose any available room under its stated restrictions
> - **(c)** Room Y is the only available room eligible to be chosen
> - **(d)** Room Y costs less than every conference room in the building
> - **(e)** Room X's better projection equipment makes it less accessible than Room Y

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> The biconditional makes the two conditions both necessary and jointly sufficient for eligibility. X fails the budget condition; Y satisfies both and is therefore eligible. Since these are the only available rooms, Y alone is eligible, as C states. Eligibility does not require the committee actually to choose Y. The premises neither compare Y with every room nor deny X's accessibility.

#### D.5.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A program performed well on test cases created by the same team that designed it. Those cases cover every documented input format, but independent users routinely submit undocumented combinations unlike the team's cases. Because the reported error rate measures only performance on the team's cases, \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** the design team intentionally concealed errors involving undocumented input combinations
> - **(b)** the program will fail on every input submitted by an independent user
> - **(c)** test cases created by a program's designers are never useful for estimating performance
> - **(d)** the reported error rate may not predict performance on the unlike inputs independent users actually submit
> - **(e)** independent users should be prohibited from submitting undocumented combinations of inputs

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> The test set differs systematically from the intended use, so performance measured on it may not generalize to independent inputs. D expresses that cautious conclusion. The premises establish neither misconduct nor universal failure, and they do not justify condemning all internal tests or restricting users.

#### D.5.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *A committee agreed in advance to adopt a new scheduling system if a representative pilot reduced average delays without increasing the rate of scheduling errors. The completed pilot was representative: average delays fell by 18 percent, while the error rate remained unchanged. No additional adoption condition was specified. Therefore, \_\_\_\_.*
>
> Which choice most logically completes the argument?
> - **(a)** the scheduling system will eliminate every delay after full adoption
> - **(b)** the pilot proves that the system is better than the old one in every respect
> - **(c)** the scheduling-error rate will decline after the system is adopted
> - **(d)** the committee must add a cost condition before it can apply its prior decision rule
> - **(e)** the committee should adopt the new scheduling system under its agreed rule

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> **Why E completes the argument:** The committee adopted an advance decision rule:
> $$(R \land \neg I)\rightarrow A,$$
> where $R$ is reduced average delay, $I$ is an increased error rate, and $A$ is adoption. The representative pilot reduced delays by 18% and left the error rate unchanged, so both antecedent conditions are satisfied. Because no other condition was specified, applying the agreed rule yields adoption.
>
> A and B overstate what one pilot shows. C predicts a decline even though the observed rate was merely unchanged. D asks the committee to add a condition after the agreed trigger has been satisfied. E is the appropriately limited conclusion.

### E.1 Causal Evaluation

In one sentence, state the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

#### E.1.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Students who enabled calendar reminders submitted more work on time than students who did not. Thus enabling calendar reminders causes timely submission.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** The comparison shows association, not causation; already-organized students may be more likely both to enable reminders and to submit on time.
>
> The conclusion is that enabling reminders causes more timely submission; the evidence is an observed difference between students who chose to enable them and those who did not. Selection prevents a causal inference: students who are already organized may be more likely both to enable reminders and to submit on time. A randomized study assigning otherwise comparable students to reminder and no-reminder conditions, followed by a meaningful difference in on-time submission, would strengthen the claim. Finding the same preexisting submission gap before reminders were available, or finding no change when reminders are randomly enabled, would weaken it.

#### E.1.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Employees who used the optional standing desks completed more support tickets per week than employees who used ordinary desks. The standing desks therefore increased productivity.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Desk choice was self-selected, so stronger or more energetic employees may be more likely both to choose standing desks and to complete more tickets.
>
> **Causal conclusion:** Using a standing desk causes an employee to complete more support tickets per week.
>
> **Evidence offered:** In an observational comparison, employees who chose the optional standing desks completed more weekly tickets than employees using ordinary desks. This establishes an association, not yet a causal effect.
>
> **Why causation does not follow:** Desk choice was not randomly assigned. The two groups may differ before desk use, and ticket count may not measure comparable work. For example, employees with stronger prior performance or greater energy may be more likely to request standing desks. Alternatively, standing-desk users may receive a larger number of easy tickets. Either explanation can produce the observed association even if the desks have no effect.
>
> **Evidence that would strengthen:** Randomly assign otherwise comparable employees to desk types, keep work volume and case difficulty similar, measure baseline and later performance, and find a sustained increase only after assignment to standing desks. A crossover design, in which the same employees use both arrangements in randomized periods, would also reduce person-level confounding.
>
> **Evidence that would weaken:** The standing-desk group already completed more tickets before receiving the desks; the difference disappears after controlling for case difficulty and hours worked; or randomized desk assignment produces no meaningful difference. Any of these findings breaks or seriously weakens the proposed causal explanation.

#### E.1.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> After brighter lamps were installed in Riverside Park, reported thefts there fell by 30 percent. The brighter lamps caused the reduction in theft.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** A before-and-after change does not isolate the lamps' effect; added patrols, seasonal attendance, or changed reporting could explain the decline.
>
> The conclusion attributes the fall in reported theft to the lamps; the evidence is a before-after change in one park. Other changes at the same time—additional patrols, seasonal attendance, construction, or changed reporting—could explain the reduction. A staggered or randomized lighting change across comparable parks, with theft declining specifically when each park receives brighter lamps, would strengthen the claim. A similar decline in unmodified parks, or records showing patrols increased at Riverside when the lamps changed, would weaken it.

#### E.1.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Patients who attended the clinic's nutrition workshop had lower blood pressure three months later than patients who skipped it. Attending the workshop lowers blood pressure.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Attendance was voluntary, so health-motivated patients may both attend the workshop and make other changes that lower blood pressure.
>
> The conclusion says workshop attendance lowers blood pressure; the evidence is the later difference between voluntary attendees and nonattendees. Attendees may have been more motivated, taken medication more consistently, exercised more, or begun with different blood pressure. Random assignment to an invitation with high compliance, or a matched study showing comparable baselines and no other treatment difference, would strengthen the claim. A large baseline difference or evidence that medication changes fully explain the later gap would weaken it.

#### E.1.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Classes using weekly practice quizzes earned higher final-exam scores than classes without them. Weekly practice quizzes improve final-exam performance.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** The classes were not otherwise controlled; stronger students, a different instructor, or extra review could explain the score difference.
>
> The conclusion is that weekly practice quizzes improve performance; the evidence compares different classes. The classes may differ in instructor, student preparation, time on task, grading incentives, or curriculum. Randomly assigning comparable course sections to otherwise identical quiz and no-quiz designs would strengthen causation, especially if the improvement tracks quiz exposure. Evidence that quiz sections had stronger incoming students or additional review time that explains the score difference would weaken the claim.

#### E.1.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Neighborhoods with more street trees had lower summer electricity use. Planting street trees reduces household electricity consumption.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** The neighborhood comparison is confounded; building age, home size, climate, or household habits could explain the lower electricity use.
>
> The conclusion says planting trees reduces electricity use; the evidence is a cross-neighborhood correlation. Tree-rich neighborhoods may also have newer buildings, different household incomes, smaller homes, cooler locations, or residents with different energy habits. Comparing randomly or quasi-randomly selected blocks before and after a tree-planting program against similar untreated blocks would strengthen the claim. If the association disappears after building age and home size are controlled, or if new trees produce no later change, the claim is weakened.

#### E.1.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Users who turned off social-media notifications reported less stress one month later than users who left notifications on. Turning off notifications reduces stress.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Turning notifications off was self-selected, so people actively trying to reduce stress may also have changed sleep, screen time, or other habits.
>
> **Causal conclusion:** Turning off social-media notifications causes a reduction in stress.
>
> **Evidence offered:** One month later, users who chose to turn notifications off reported less stress than users who left them on. This is an association between a self-selected behavior and a later self-reported outcome.
>
> **Why causation does not follow:** Users chose their settings. People actively trying to reduce stress may simultaneously improve sleep, reduce workload, leave stressful platforms, or limit total screen time. Baseline stress or notification volume may also differ between groups. In addition, reported stress can be influenced by expectations about the intervention.
>
> **Evidence that would strengthen:** Measure baseline stress, randomly assign users to notification-on and notification-off conditions, verify compliance, hold other social-media access comparable, and find a larger reduction in the off group. Replication with both self-report and a well-chosen behavioral or physiological measure would further strengthen the case.
>
> **Evidence that would weaken:** The off group already reported less stress at baseline; most members also sharply reduced total social-media use while the on group did not; the association vanishes after those differences are controlled; or a randomized study finds no difference. Those results suggest selection or another behavioral change, rather than notification status itself, explains the outcome.

#### E.1.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Plants given the new fertilizer were taller after six weeks than plants grown without it. The new fertilizer makes plants grow taller.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** The groups may differ in more than fertilizer; light, water, soil, or starting plant size could explain the height difference.
>
> The conclusion is that the fertilizer causes greater height; the evidence is a difference between treated and untreated plants. The inference remains uncertain unless assignment and other conditions were controlled: the treated plants might have received more light, water, or started larger. Randomly assigning similar seedlings to fertilizer and control groups while holding soil, light, and water constant would strengthen the claim. A baseline height imbalance, unequal light, or a blinded replication showing no height difference would weaken it.

#### E.1.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The software team adopted daily stand-up meetings in March. Its defect-resolution time declined in April. Daily stand-ups made the team resolve defects faster.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** The before-and-after comparison does not isolate stand-ups; easier defects, new staff, a new tool, or backlog clearance could explain the faster resolutions.
>
> The conclusion credits stand-ups for faster resolution; the evidence is a short before-after sequence. April defects may have been easier, the team may have gained staff, a new diagnostic tool may have arrived, or the March backlog may have cleared. Comparing similar teams with staggered adoption, or tracking the same team over a longer stable period while other changes are documented, would strengthen the claim. Evidence that defect complexity fell sharply or that comparable teams improved without stand-ups would weaken it.

#### E.1.10


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Residents who used home water filters reported fewer stomach illnesses than residents who did not. Using a home water filter prevents stomach illness.
> **Short response:** State the causal flaw and one plausible alternative explanation.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Filter use was self-selected, so water source, hygiene, health, or reporting differences could explain the illness difference.
>
> The conclusion is that filter use prevents illness; the evidence is a self-selected user comparison based on reports. Filter users may have different water sources, hygiene practices, income, travel, or willingness to report illness. A randomized filter trial with the same water source and prespecified illness measurement would strengthen the causal claim. Tests showing both groups received equally clean water while reporting habits differed, or adjustment for water source eliminating the association, would weaken it.

### E.2 Apply a Principle

Give the answer letter and one sentence naming the decisive condition match.

#### E.2.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: A school should not impose a serious burden that affected students cannot reasonably avoid merely to obtain a minor administrative benefit, especially when a substantially less burdensome practical alternative would obtain that benefit.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A school may require laboratory goggles because preventing serious eye injuries is more important than preserving students' choice of eyewear, although inexpensive loaners are available.
> - **(b)** A school should not require every student to buy a costly new device solely to simplify attendance entry when existing identification cards would work nearly as well and no loan program is offered.
> - **(c)** A school should retain a slightly slower registration system because any inconvenience to students outweighs every possible administrative benefit.
> - **(d)** A school should make an optional scheduling application available even though some students lack compatible phones, because students may continue to use the website.
> - **(e)** A school should reject a device requirement whenever even one student dislikes it, regardless of its cost, avoidability, alternatives, or expected benefit.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Expected short response:** B — the purchase is costly and unavoidable, serves only convenience, and has a less burdensome practical alternative.
>
> Choice B supplies every condition: the purchase is costly and unavoidable, its benefit is only administrative convenience, and existing cards are a less burdensome practical alternative. Choice A concerns a serious safety benefit and offers loaners; D is avoidable; C and E treat burden or dislike as decisive without the principle's other conditions.

#### E.2.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: If an institution publicly promises to decide eligible submissions by a stated criterion, it should not replace that criterion with an unrelated one merely because the result would be unpopular; this does not require using a criterion that would make the institution violate a legal or safety obligation.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A museum that promised to select eligible works by artistic merit should not reject a safe, lawful sculpture that ranks highest on merit solely because trustees expect its message to be unpopular.
> - **(b)** A museum may exclude the highest-rated sculpture when its dimensions violate the safety limits stated in the call for submissions.
> - **(c)** A museum should display a lower-rated work when it expects that work to sell more tickets, even though artistic merit was the announced criterion, because revenue is relevant to every museum decision.
> - **(d)** A museum must use artistic merit forever after announcing it once, even for later exhibitions advertised under different criteria.
> - **(e)** A museum may secretly change from artistic merit to donor preference provided that it makes the change before the judges submit their scores.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> **Expected short response:** A — the submission is eligible, safe, and lawful, but the panel substitutes popularity for its promised merit criterion.
>
> Choice A concerns an eligible, safe, lawful submission, the publicly promised merit criterion, and an attempted substitution based only on unpopularity. Choice B applies an announced safety limit, while C and E endorse unrelated substitutions and D wrongly extends one promise to future decisions.

#### E.2.03


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: A person who voluntarily creates or increases a foreseeable risk should take a reasonable precaution to protect people who cannot readily detect the risk, unless the precaution would itself create a comparably serious danger.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A tenant should warn a landlord about a loose stair the tenant noticed, although the tenant neither caused nor increased the defect and every visitor can see it.
> - **(b)** A cyclist should replace a worn tire before riding alone on a closed course because the cyclist created the tire's manufacturing defect.
> - **(c)** A restaurant should eliminate every possible kitchen hazard even when the proposed precaution would block its only fire exit.
> - **(d)** A contractor who removes a floor panel should place a visible barrier around the resulting opening because approaching visitors cannot see it and the barrier creates no comparable danger.
> - **(e)** A homeowner should repair a plainly visible cracked walkway whenever repair is possible, whether or not the homeowner created the crack or anyone has difficulty detecting it.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Expected short response:** D — removing the panel creates a foreseeable hidden risk, and a visible barrier is a reasonable precaution without comparable danger.
>
> Choice D maps each element: removing the panel creates a foreseeable risk, visitors cannot readily detect it, and a visible barrier is a reasonable precaution with no comparable danger. The other choices omit creation, an exposed person, hiddenness, or the exception.

#### E.2.04


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: When officials have good evidence that two feasible policies would achieve the same important benefit, they should choose the one that restricts individual choice less, unless it would cost substantially more or create a distinct serious risk.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A city should choose a voluntary rebate because it restricts choice less, even though studies predict that it will achieve only half the pollution reduction of the feasible mandate.
> - **(b)** A city should choose a voluntary rebate whenever it is popular, without comparing effectiveness, feasibility, cost, or safety.
> - **(c)** A city should choose a voluntary rebate over a mandatory replacement program when controlled pilots show equal pollution reduction, both are feasible and similarly priced, and neither creates an additional safety risk.
> - **(d)** A city should choose a voluntary rebate that costs four times as much as the equally effective mandate because less restriction always controls.
> - **(e)** A city should choose a rebate and ignore evidence that its disposal method creates a serious fire risk absent from the equally effective mandate.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> **Expected short response:** C — both policies are feasible and equally effective, while the rebate is less restrictive with no cost or safety exception.
>
> **Why C is correct:** Map every condition in the principle:
> - **Same important benefit:** controlled pilots show equal pollution reduction.
> - **Both feasible:** the choice explicitly says both policies are feasible.
> - **Less restrictive:** a voluntary rebate restricts individual choice less than a mandate.
> - **No substantial-cost exception:** they are similarly priced.
> - **No distinct-risk exception:** neither creates an additional safety risk.
>
> With every condition satisfied and neither exception triggered, the principle directs officials to choose the rebate. A fails the equal-benefit condition. B supplies no comparison of effectiveness, cost, or risk. D triggers the substantially-higher-cost exception. E triggers the distinct-serious-risk exception. The principle is conditional, so “less restrictive” does not automatically control in those cases.

#### E.2.05


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: A positive result from a method known to produce many false positives should not by itself justify a serious penalty, though it may justify a proportionate investigation or help support a penalty when reliable independent evidence corroborates it.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A university should ignore every alert from an error-prone plagiarism detector, including when deciding whether an instructor should compare the papers manually.
> - **(b)** A university may expel a student after an error-prone detector flags a paper, provided the vendor calls its output evidence rather than a conclusion.
> - **(c)** A university may expel a student when the only evidence is two alerts produced by the same error-prone detector on the same paper.
> - **(d)** A university must refuse to consider a detector alert even after an independent document history and the student's admissions reliably corroborate it.
> - **(e)** A university should not expel a student solely because one error-prone detector flags a paper, although the alert may prompt a fair review for independent evidence.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: E
> **Expected short response:** E — a false-positive-prone result may support a proportionate investigation but not a serious penalty without independent evidence.
>
> Choice E distinguishes the prohibited serious penalty based solely on a false-positive-prone result from the permitted proportionate investigation. A wrongly bars investigation, B and C treat labels or repeated dependent alerts as corroboration, and D ignores the independent-evidence qualification.

#### E.2.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: Before participating in a decision, a decision-maker should disclose a personal financial relationship that could reasonably cause an informed observer to doubt the decision-maker's impartiality; a remote interest shared in roughly equal measure with the general public does not by itself require disclosure.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A reviewer whose sibling owns one of three companies bidding for a contract should disclose that relationship before scoring the bids, even if the reviewer believes the scoring will be fair.
> - **(b)** A city council member must disclose owning the same broad index fund held by millions of residents before voting on an unrelated park schedule.
> - **(c)** A reviewer may wait until after bids are scored to disclose a direct investment in the winning company because disclosure cannot change the written scores.
> - **(d)** A reviewer should disclose disliking one proposal's font because every personal reaction is a financial relationship.
> - **(e)** A council member need not disclose a consulting contract with a developer seeking the council's approval if the member promises to use objective criteria.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: A
> **Expected short response:** A — the personal financial relationship gives reasonable grounds for doubt and must be disclosed before participation.
>
> Choice A supplies a personal financial relationship tied to the pending decision, reasonable grounds for doubt, and disclosure before participation. B fits the general-public exception, C is late, D is not financial, and E treats claimed objectivity as eliminating the duty.

#### E.2.07


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: When a temporary restriction is justified solely by an emergency risk, officials should end it once reliable current evidence shows that risk has ceased, unless they publicly establish an independent lawful justification for continuing it.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A trail closed for wildfire danger must reopen on its scheduled date even though current fire maps still show the danger that justified closure.
> - **(b)** A trail should remain closed after wildfire danger ends because visitors have become accustomed to using another trail, although officials identify no new legal basis.
> - **(c)** A trail closed for wildfire danger should remain closed permanently whenever reopening might create any ordinary hiking risk.
> - **(d)** A trail closed solely because of active wildfire danger should reopen after reliable inspection confirms that danger has ceased and officials identify no independent basis for closure.
> - **(e)** A trail closed for wildfire danger must reopen after the fire is contained even when officials publicly establish that a newly discovered bridge failure independently makes the trail unsafe.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: D
> **Expected short response:** D — reliable evidence shows the restriction's sole emergency basis has ended, and no independent justification remains.
>
> Choice D identifies the restriction's sole emergency basis, reliable evidence that the specific risk has ceased, and no independent justification. A lacks cessation, B and C invent no qualifying basis, and E ignores the independent bridge-safety justification.

#### E.2.08


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: If a person knowingly accepts a divisible benefit from a voluntary cooperative practice while deliberately refusing a fair share of its ordinary costs, the other participants may require that person either to contribute or to forgo the benefit, provided forgoing it is a practical option.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** Roommates may charge a visitor for shared internet the visitor neither requested nor used because everyone in the apartment benefits from cooperation.
> - **(b)** A roommate who knowingly uses optional shared internet but deliberately refuses an equal share of its reasonable bill may be asked to pay or use an available private connection instead.
> - **(c)** Roommates may require a resident to pay for emergency structural repairs or leave immediately, even when leaving is not practical and the safety benefit cannot be divided.
> - **(d)** A roommate who paid the agreed share of internet costs may be barred from using it because the other roommates later decide that equal shares were unfair.
> - **(e)** Roommates may enroll everyone in a premium service and demand equal payment even from a resident who declined it in advance and receives no separable benefit.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: B
> **Expected short response:** B — the person knowingly accepts a divisible voluntary benefit, refuses a fair ordinary cost, and can contribute or forgo the benefit.
>
> Choice B includes knowing acceptance of a divisible voluntary benefit, deliberate refusal of a fair ordinary cost, and a practical contribute-or-forgo remedy. A and E lack accepted benefit, C lacks divisibility and a practical exit, and D lacks refusal to contribute.

#### E.2.09


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> *Principle: A rule adopted to prevent a specified harm should not prohibit conduct shown not to create or increase that harm merely because the conduct resembles harmful conduct in a way irrelevant to the rule's purpose.*
>
> Which judgment most directly applies the principle? Give the letter and one short sentence naming the decisive condition match.
> - **(a)** A park rule aimed at preventing soil damage should allow every kind of equipment whose owner promises to be careful, even if tests show that some equipment increases erosion.
> - **(b)** A park should repeal its soil-protection rule because protecting soil is less important than allowing every visitor the same equipment choices.
> - **(c)** A park rule aimed at preventing soil damage should permit lightweight tripods that field tests show do not increase such damage, although they share three legs and a camera mount with the heavy rigs that do.
> - **(d)** A park may ban a lightweight tripod solely because it is expensive, even though expense is unrelated to soil damage, because rules need not track their announced purposes.
> - **(e)** A park rule should permit a heavy camera rig whenever it looks different from the equipment that originally caused erosion, even if it creates the same soil damage.

> [!NOTE]- Answer: C
> **Expected short response:** C — testing shows lightweight tripods do not cause the soil damage the rule targets, so superficial resemblance is not a relevant basis for prohibition.
>
> **Why C is correct:** The rule’s specified purpose is preventing soil damage. Field tests show that lightweight tripods do not increase that harm. Their resemblance to harmful heavy rigs—three legs and a camera mount—is irrelevant to soil damage. Prohibiting them merely because of that resemblance would therefore violate the principle, so the rule should permit them.
>
> A substitutes an owner’s promise for evidence and would permit equipment known to increase erosion. B rejects the rule based on a different value judgment rather than applying its stated purpose. D permits a ban for expense, which is unrelated to the specified harm and therefore contradicts the principle. E focuses on superficial difference while ignoring evidence that the heavy rig creates the same harm.

### E.3 Integrated Op-Ed Revision

State the main conclusion and one revision whose strength matches the evidence.

#### E.3.01


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> Some students share rooms or equipment and lack a dependable place to run long programs at home. A pilot last spring kept one lab open late, and students used it steadily. Any resource that is steadily used is worth its cost. Students who used the pilot also completed more assignments on time than students who did not. Thus the university should keep one computer lab open overnight during the final two weeks, since the pilot proves that overnight access improves academic performance.
> - **(a)** State the main conclusion.
> - **(b)** Give one evidence-supported revision of that conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: keep one computer lab open overnight during the final two weeks. Revision: the university should run an extended-hours trial and measure demand, costs, and comparable student outcomes before deciding on overnight access.
>
> **Main conclusion:** The university should keep one computer lab open overnight during the final two weeks.
>
> **Strongest stated support:** Some students lack dependable computing space at home; students steadily used a late-hours pilot; and pilot users submitted more assignments on time than nonusers. The first two facts support the existence of an access problem and demand for later hours. The submission data give a reason to study a possible academic benefit.
>
> **Ambiguity:** “Academic performance” might mean on-time submission, completion, grades, learning, retention, or something else. The evidence measures only on-time submission. “Worth its cost” is also underspecified: steady use alone does not show how large the benefit is or whether it justifies staffing, security, and facility costs.
>
> **Inferential gaps:** A lab kept open “late” does not establish demand for **overnight** access. The correlation between using the lab and timely submission does not prove that access caused the difference. The argument also applies the rule “any steadily used resource is worth its cost” without evidence that this very broad rule is reliable.
>
> **Alternative explanation:** More organized or motivated students may be more likely both to use the pilot and to submit work on time. Pilot users may also differ in course load, deadlines, housing, or prior achievement.
>
> **Supported revision:** *The pilot demonstrates demand for later lab access among students who lack dependable computing space. The university should therefore run a finals-period extended-hours trial and measure hourly use, staffing and security costs, and outcomes for comparable students before deciding whether overnight operation is warranted.*
>
> This conclusion preserves the genuine access evidence, avoids equating association with causation, and recommends a next step proportionate to what the pilot established.

#### E.3.02


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement

> - **(b)** Give one evidence-supported revision of that conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: permanently remove every downtown parking fee. Revision: compare several limited free-parking trials with similar paid periods while measuring sales, traffic, access, and revenue before changing permanent policy.
>
> The main conclusion is that every downtown parking fee should be removed permanently. Its strongest support is the simultaneous weekend increase in sales and temporary free parking. 'Good for the whole community' is ambiguous, and one weekend neither isolates parking's effect nor establishes that permanent removal has net benefits after congestion and lost revenue. A festival, favorable weather, or seasonal shopping could explain sales. A careful revision is: 'Because sales rose during the free-parking weekend, the city should compare several limited free-parking trials with similar paid-parking periods while measuring sales, traffic, access, and revenue before changing permanent policy.'

#### E.3.06


> [!QUESTION]- Full question statement
> The new introductory textbook has shorter chapters than the old one. Students in the first class to use it completed more assigned reading, and no instructor wants students to fall behind. The department should require the new textbook in every section because shorter books cause deeper learning.
> - **(a)** State the main conclusion.
> - **(b)** Give one evidence-supported revision of that conclusion.

> [!NOTE]- Solution
> **Expected short response:** Main: require the new textbook in every introductory section. Revision: pilot it in additional comparable sections and measure completion, comprehension, cost, accessibility, and instructor practices before adopting it coursewide.
>
> **Main conclusion:** The department should require the new textbook in every introductory section.
>
> **Strongest stated support:** The new book has shorter chapters, and students in the first section using it completed more assigned reading. This provides preliminary evidence that the book may make completion easier in at least one setting.
>
> **Ambiguity:** “Shorter books” could mean a lower total page count, shorter individual chapters, less content, or more concise presentation. Only shorter chapters were established. “Deeper learning” is also undefined and was not measured; reading completion is not the same as comprehension, transfer, or long-term retention.
>
> **Inferential gaps:** The argument moves from one section to every section and from correlation to causation. It also moves from increased completion to deeper learning without evidence connecting the two. The statement that no instructor wants students to fall behind expresses a shared goal, but it does not show that this book best achieves it.
>
> **Alternative explanation:** The instructor may have changed reminders, quizzes, pacing, workload, or grading incentives. The first class may also differ from earlier classes in preparation or motivation. Any of those differences could increase completion without the textbook causing it.
>
> **Supported revision:** *Because reading completion was higher in the first section using the new textbook, the department should pilot it in additional comparable sections and measure completion, comprehension, cost, accessibility, and instructor practices before deciding whether to adopt it coursewide.*
>
> The revision treats the observed result as promising evidence, not proof of deeper learning or a sufficient basis for an immediate universal requirement.
