\begin{enumerate}
% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q1 -- flaw: anecdote versus general claim.
\item A transit commentator argues: ``Researchers say unpredictable bus arrivals reduce riders' willingness to use transit. That claim is plainly exaggerated. My bus arrived late three times last month, yet I renewed my annual pass.''

The commentator's reasoning is weakest because it
\begin{enumerate}
\item assumes that annual passes are less expensive than monthly passes
\item never establishes that the researchers studied buses rather than trains
\item treats reduced willingness as though it meant that every rider must stop riding
\item overlooks the possibility that the commentator enjoys waiting outdoors
\item uses one rider's experience to dismiss a claim about riders generally
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q2 -- method: analogy rebuts a novelty claim.
\item A council member calls the city's proposed tool-lending program an unprecedented expansion of library services. A librarian replies that city libraries have lent musical instruments and science kits for years. The librarian concludes that lending tools would not be a radical departure.

The librarian's argument works primarily by
\begin{enumerate}
\item showing that the proposed program would save more money than it costs
\item denying that tools require any maintenance
\item comparing the proposal with established lending practices
\item arguing that every useful public service belongs in a library
\item questioning the council member's knowledge of tools
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q3 -- point at issue.
\item Mira says that the archive should prohibit all visitor photography because bright flashes can damage delicate pigments. Dev agrees that flashes can be harmful but notes that phones can be set to photograph without a flash; Dev therefore opposes a blanket prohibition.

Mira and Dev disagree about whether
\begin{enumerate}
\item bright flashes can damage delicate pigments
\item the archive should prohibit every form of visitor photography
\item phones are capable of taking photographs without a flash
\item the archive contains objects with delicate pigments
\item visitors sometimes forget to disable a flash
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q4 -- resolve a discrepancy.
\item A new coating was shown in laboratory trials not to alter seed germination. Nevertheless, in the first commercial season, farms planting coated seed reported substantially lower germination than farms planting uncoated seed.

Which fact would best reconcile these observations?
\begin{enumerate}
\item The coating made the seeds easier to distinguish by color.
\item Some farms plant more than one crop each season.
\item Coated seed cost slightly more per kilogram.
\item Before delivery, the first commercial batch of coated seed was stored in an overheated warehouse.
\item Most farmers had heard about the laboratory trials.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q5 -- weaken a proposed diagnostic.
\item Researchers found that tired typists paused longer before correcting errors than rested typists did. They conclude that software measuring correction pauses could become a reliable detector of fatigue.

Which fact most weakens the conclusion?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Some tired typists make fewer errors than others.
\item The study used the same keyboard model for every participant.
\item Typists can improve their speed with practice.
\item Fatigue can result from either sleep loss or illness.
\item Rested typists working under time pressure show the same pause pattern as tired typists.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q6 -- necessary causal assumption.
\item A town removed lighting from several pedestrian crossings to reduce energy use. Collisions at those crossings then increased sharply. An advocate concludes that the lighting policy caused a serious safety problem.

The advocate's conclusion requires assuming that
\begin{enumerate}
\item every collision occurred after sunset
\item the town intended to make walking less safe
\item energy conservation is never worth any risk
\item without the lighting removal, the increase would have been substantially smaller
\item drivers knew why the lights had been removed
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q7 -- strengthen a correlation explanation.
\item A reviewer observes that bicycle mechanics with the longest waiting lists also receive the highest customer-satisfaction ratings. She takes this as evidence that customers can recognize excellent repair work rather than merely following fashion.

Which fact most strengthens her interpretation?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Mechanics' waiting-list length usually grows after independent inspections document unusually accurate repairs.
\item Some excellent mechanics have recently opened shops and have short waiting lists.
\item Popular mechanics tend to work in densely populated neighborhoods.
\item Customers dislike leaving bicycles at a shop overnight.
\item A mechanic can repair several kinds of bicycle.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q8 -- complete with supporting premise.
\item Shells of a freshwater snail were found in a layer deposited when a present-day salt marsh was an inland lake. The snail now lives only in nearby springs with very low salinity. The lake was probably much less salty when that layer formed because \underline{\hspace{1.6in}}.

Which completion provides the strongest support?
\begin{enumerate}
\item the springs are colder than the modern marsh
\item the shell layer also contains windblown sand
\item the snail cannot survive or reproduce outside a narrow low-salinity range
\item several other snail species live in both fresh and salty water
\item the lake existed for thousands of years
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q9 -- resolve a discrepancy.
\item Surveys show that buyers rank quiet operation as their highest priority when choosing a desk fan. Yet several independently measured models with the lowest noise levels sell poorly even among buyers who report that priority.

Which fact best explains the result?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Buyers say quiet operation matters more than the color of a fan.
\item Many buyers rely on retailer noise labels that have not been updated after recent model redesigns.
\item Quiet fans tend to use slightly less electricity.
\item Some noisy fans are inexpensive.
\item Most buyers use fans only during warm months.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q10 -- quantitative inference.
\item Most members of a twelve-person committee believe that they attended fewer meetings than most other members. It is impossible for most members actually to have attended fewer meetings than most other members. Therefore,
\begin{enumerate}
\item every member attended exactly the same number of meetings
\item attendance records must have been destroyed
\item most members attended fewer than half of the meetings
\item no member knows how many meetings anyone attended
\item at least some members hold an inaccurate comparative belief
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q11 -- weaken a policy recommendation.
\item A mayor proposes putting independent muralists on the city payroll. The mayor reasons that paid muralists will avoid criticizing the city, while residents will still receive the street art they enjoy. Thus the plan will both reduce criticism and satisfy the public.

Which fact most weakens the proposal's reasoning?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Some murals require expensive weather-resistant paint.
\item Other cities sponsor annual art festivals.
\item Most murals neither praise nor criticize municipal policy.
\item Residents strongly prefer independently produced murals and avoid art identified as official city messaging.
\item The city employs designers for its public notices.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q12 -- supported conditional inference.
\item Trail stewards use radios to report riders operating motorcycles in protected areas, allowing rangers to arrive before the riders leave. Riders avoid sectors they know the stewards monitor. Stewards nevertheless continue to encounter and report riders in remote sectors.

Which statement is best supported?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Most riders know every sector the stewards monitor.
\item Rangers patrol remote sectors continuously.
\item Some riders enter sectors without knowing that stewards monitor them.
\item Radios are the most effective conservation technology.
\item Every steward has personally encountered an illegal rider.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q13 -- counterexample to a definition.
\item A theorist defines creativity as producing something new in order to win approval. Suppose, however, that an isolated mathematician develops a genuinely new proof solely from curiosity. We would still call the work creative. The definition is therefore inadequate.

The argument challenges the definition by
\begin{enumerate}
\item claiming that approval is impossible to measure
\item showing that everything new is automatically valuable
\item presenting a plausible creative case that lacks the stated motive
\item arguing that curiosity and approval are identical motives
\item appealing to the theorist's lack of mathematical expertise
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q14 -- supporting principle.
\item Autonomous delivery carts may share crowded sidewalks with pedestrians. Even one poorly tested control system could cause injuries. A planner therefore argues that no new cart model should operate in public until rigorous safety trials have been completed.

Which principle most directly supports the planner?
\begin{enumerate}
\item A device shown to be dangerous should be repaired rather than tested again.
\item A technology that may impose serious public risk should not be deployed before adequate safety testing.
\item Any technology that passes one laboratory test should be approved permanently.
\item Public inconvenience always outweighs private innovation.
\item Only government laboratories can conduct reliable safety trials.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q15 -- criticizing one argument is not disproving its conclusion.
\item A director argues that a historical archive should digitize its catalog because ``an archive is simply a warehouse for data, and every modern warehouse uses digital inventory.'' A critic replies that an archive is not a warehouse and concludes that digitizing the catalog would be a mistake.

The critic's reasoning is defective because it
\begin{enumerate}
\item appeals to the popularity of digital inventory
\item assumes that historical records never need preservation
\item treats two different institutions as identical
\item attacks the director's character rather than the proposal
\item rejects the proposal merely because one argument for it is inadequate
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q16 -- strengthen culture versus genetics.
\item Five island colonies of one finch subspecies and two mainland colonies of another were observed while opening hard seedpods. One island colony used the same technique as the mainland colonies rather than the technique used by the other island colonies. Researchers infer that the technique is learned rather than genetically fixed.

Which finding most strengthens that inference?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Young finches raised by foster colonies adopt the foster colony's technique rather than their biological parents' technique.
\item Mainland seedpods are slightly larger than island seedpods.
\item Each colony uses more than one kind of food.
\item The two subspecies have different feather markings.
\item Some finches fail to open any seedpod.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q17 -- necessary assumption about voluntary choice.
\item An ethicist argues that disclosure and extra pay do not by themselves justify assigning couriers to unusually hazardous routes. A courier facing severe financial pressure may accept an assignment under duress, and consent under duress is not fully voluntary.

The argument requires which assumption?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Hazardous routes should never be used.
\item Extra pay makes every risky choice involuntary.
\item A disclosed and compensated choice can still be made under duress.
\item Couriers do not understand written disclosures.
\item Any voluntary transaction is morally acceptable.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q18 -- contraposition.
\item Any properly calibrated humidity sensor exposed for no more than two hours will continue to function. Sensor R was exposed for ninety minutes and then malfunctioned.

Which conclusion must follow?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Sensor R was not properly calibrated.
\item Every uncalibrated sensor malfunctions within two hours.
\item Humidity never damages a calibrated sensor.
\item Sensor R would have worked after three hours.
\item The ninety-minute measurement was inaccurate.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q19 -- circular price-quality reasoning.
\item A tutoring company claims its service is the best because it charges the highest fee. Its spokesperson adds that any equally good competitor would necessarily charge at least as much.

The reasoning is flawed because it
\begin{enumerate}
\item confuses the best current service with the best imaginable service
\item infers a property of the company from a property of each tutor
\item gives premises that contradict one another
\item assumes the link between high price and high quality that the conclusion needs
\item ignores the possibility that all tutoring is ineffective
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q20 -- discovery and patent rights.
\item Naturally occurring crystal lattices should not be patentable merely because a laboratory first identifies them. Identifying a structure that already exists is discovery, not invention.

Which principle best supports the conclusion?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every invention should immediately enter the public domain.
\item Mere discovery of an existing thing does not create a right to patent that thing.
\item Laboratories should receive credit for publishing discoveries.
\item A profitable discovery is always an invention.
\item Patents are justified only when research is expensive.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q21 -- affirming the consequent.
\item Any volunteer who attends the entire safety workshop understands the emergency protocol. Several volunteers understand the protocol. Therefore, several volunteers must have attended the entire workshop.

The conclusion fails because it remains possible that
\begin{enumerate}
\item no volunteer who attended the workshop understood the protocol
\item the workshop covered topics besides emergencies
\item those volunteers learned the protocol from the manual without attending the entire workshop
\item every volunteer attended at least part of the workshop
\item some volunteers disliked the instructor
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q22 -- classification rule.
\item Careful record keeping is a professional virtue. No characteristic that tends to mislead colleagues qualifies as a professional virtue. Some professional virtues also benefit the person who has them.

Which statement must be true?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Careful record keeping does not tend to mislead colleagues.
\item Every characteristic that helps colleagues is a professional virtue.
\item Careful record keeping always benefits its practitioner.
\item Anyone who lacks careful record keeping lacks every professional virtue.
\item No professional virtue benefits its practitioner.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q23 -- parallel temporal projection.
\item Last winter, none of the seedlings kept under a particular warming cover suffered frost damage. This winter every seedling in Greenhouse K will use that cover. Therefore, no seedling in Greenhouse K will suffer frost damage this winter.

Which argument uses the most similar pattern?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every parcel scanned yesterday reached the sorting room; parcel K was scanned yesterday, so parcel K reached the sorting room.
\item Last season, no bicycle using Sealant Z had a puncture; every bicycle on this season's tour uses Sealant Z, so none will have a puncture this season.
\item No metal sample with coating R corroded in the same laboratory trial; sample M had coating R in that trial, so it did not corrode.
\item All books borrowed on Monday were returned; every book now on the cart was borrowed Monday, so every book on the cart was returned.
\item No nocturnal bird feeds at noon; this bird is nocturnal, so it does not feed at noon.
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q24 -- desire versus induced belief.
\item Advertisements are often accused of creating people's goals. Yet buyers usually purchase a product because they believe it will advance a goal they already have; advertising chiefly tries to create that belief. Thus advertisers generally
\begin{enumerate}
\item transform every preference into a necessity
\item create goals only through false claims
\item prevent products from advancing buyers' goals
\item exploit existing goals more often than they create them
\item cause buyers to distrust every product claim
\end{enumerate}

% Source map: 2024 Feb LR Q25 -- parallel inverse-selection flaw.
\item A composer notes that several pieces praised by local musicians were rejected by a festival jury. The composer concludes that any piece accepted by that jury must lack musical merit.

Which argument most closely repeats the composer's error?
\begin{enumerate}
\item Several durable backpacks cost little, so price alone does not establish durability.
\item Two acclaimed novels were omitted from a reading list, so the list is not a complete catalog of acclaimed novels.
\item Several highly accurate forecasts were not featured on a news program, so any forecast the program features must be inaccurate.
\item Every inspected bridge passed the safety test, so at least one bridge can pass it.
\item Some applicants with extensive experience were rejected, so experience is not sufficient for acceptance.
\end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Answer Key and Concise Explanations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{E.} One unusual rider cannot establish how lateness affects riders as a population.
\item \textbf{C.} The reply answers the novelty charge by pointing to relevant existing lending programs.
\item \textbf{B.} Mira supports a universal ban; Dev rejects that policy while accepting the concern about flashes.
\item \textbf{D.} Overheating supplies an independent cause of the commercial batch's poor germination.
\item \textbf{E.} If rested typists under time pressure can produce the same signal, the signal does not reliably identify fatigue.
\item \textbf{D.} The policy can be called causal only if the increase would have been smaller without it.
\item \textbf{A.} Independent quality findings preceding demand connect long waits to repair skill rather than fashion.
\item \textbf{C.} A narrow salinity tolerance makes the snail a useful indicator of the former lake's salinity.
\item \textbf{B.} Buyers can sincerely seek quiet fans yet choose poorly when the information they use is stale.
\item \textbf{E.} The comparative beliefs cannot all be accurate, so at least some are mistaken.
\item \textbf{D.} If official sponsorship destroys the feature residents value, the plan will not satisfy its stated goal.
\item \textbf{C.} Riders encountered in monitored sectors cannot have known that those sectors were monitored, given the avoidance rule.
\item \textbf{C.} The hypothetical is a case ordinarily classified as creative that the proposed definition excludes.
\item \textbf{B.} This principle directly converts possible serious risk into a predeployment testing requirement.
\item \textbf{E.} Defeating one supporting analogy does not establish that the recommendation itself is false.
\item \textbf{A.} Foster-group adoption strongly separates learned technique from inherited ancestry.
\item \textbf{C.} Without this possibility, disclosure and compensation would answer the ethicist's concern.
\item \textbf{A.} Calibrated and briefly exposed implies functional; malfunction after brief exposure implies not calibrated.
\item \textbf{D.} Price is treated as proof of quality only by assuming that equal quality requires equal price.
\item \textbf{B.} The principle supplies the missing bridge from mere discovery to lack of patent entitlement.
\item \textbf{C.} Understanding may have another sufficient cause; the argument incorrectly reverses the stated conditional.
\item \textbf{A.} Since careful record keeping is a virtue, it cannot have the disqualifying tendency to mislead.
\item \textbf{B.} Both arguments transfer a protective result from an earlier period to a new period without supporting stability.
\item \textbf{D.} The account assigns advertising the role of shaping beliefs about how to serve goals already present.
\item \textbf{C.} Both arguments move illicitly from some meritorious cases being rejected to all accepted cases lacking merit.
\end{enumerate}
