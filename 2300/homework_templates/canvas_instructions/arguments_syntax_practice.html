Practice reading the notation and definitions used in the Arguments Core. There are 60 short statements. Read each statement as written before choosing an answer.

  • True: a complete proposition that is true.
  • False: a complete proposition that is false.
  • Not a truth-evaluable proposition as written: incomplete, ill-formed, undefined, or containing an unbound variable. This is different from False.

Given: p, q, and r denote propositions. A displayed argument lists its premises before ∴ and one conclusion after it. Γ ⊨ C means that every valuation satisfying all premises in Γ also satisfies C.

Notation: ¬ means “not”; ∧ means “and”; ∨ means inclusive “or”; p → q means “if p, then q”; Γ ⊨ C means “C follows logically from premises Γ”; ∴ means “therefore.”

Separate premises from the conclusion. Check whether the claim about the argument is complete before judging its truth; a complete claim that an invalid argument is valid is False, not the third option.
